8.1 Key relations

Forward bias a pn⁺ junction and the barrier drops, so electrons from the n⁺ side are injected into the p side. They recombine within a diffusion length of the junction and, in a direct-gap semiconductor, emit photons of energy close to the gap (slides 86–87):

\[ h\nu\approx E_g,\qquad \lambda\,[\mu\text{m}]\approx\frac{1.24}{E_g\,[\text{eV}]}. \]

Under bias the single Fermi level splits into two quasi-Fermi levels, \(E_{Fn}\) for electrons and \(E_{Fp}\) for holes, whose separation equals \(eV\) (slide 87c). The emission colour is set by bandgap engineering: alloying binaries such as GaAs and AlAs or GaN and InN shifts \(E_g\) (slides 114–115). The alloy must keep the lattice constant of its substrate, otherwise dislocations form and kill the radiative efficiency. Vegard’s law interpolates the lattice constant, \(a(x)=xa_1+(1-x)a_2\), while the bandgap often bows, \(E_g(x)=xE_1+(1-x)E_2-bx(1-x)\).

A heterojunction joins two different semiconductors (slides 93–96). Its band edges jump by \(\Delta E_c\) and \(\Delta E_v\). In a double heterostructure (DH) a thin narrow-gap active layer sits between wide-gap cladding layers. The offsets trap the injected carriers in the active layer, which raises the recombination rate. The cladding is also transparent to the emitted photons and has a lower refractive index, so it guides the light. This is the planar waveguide of Chapter 2.

8.5 Heterojunctions: carrier and photon confinement (slides 93–97)

Two numbers show why every modern LED is a heterostructure. The fraction of injected electrons that spill over a barrier \(\Delta E_c\) is about \(\exp(-\Delta E_c/k_BT)\). The fraction of the guided light that overlaps the active layer is the optical confinement factor \(\Gamma\) of the slab waveguide formed by the index step:

With \(x=0.3\) cladding, \(\Delta E_c\approx0.25\) eV and the leakage is below \(10^{-4}\). A 0.1–0.2 µm active layer confines 20–60 % of the guided light. Edge-emitting LEDs (Chapter 12) and laser diodes (Ch. 4) exploit both effects. The Np and pP heterojunction band diagrams of slides 95–96 follow the same rule as homojunctions: flat \(E_F\) at equilibrium, bands bent by the space charge, plus the offsets \(\Delta E_c\) and \(\Delta E_v\) at the interface.

8.5.1 Simulating a double heterostructure

The drift–diffusion simulator of Chapter 7 showed why a homojunction is a poor LED. The injected electrons spread over a diffusion length of several µm, so their density stays low, and the radiative rate per electron, \(BN_a\), is fixed by the doping. The panel below adds the band offsets. A p-GaAs active layer of thickness \(d\) sits between a p-Al\(_x\)Ga\(_{1-x}\)As cladding (\(N_a=3\times10^{18}\) cm⁻³) and an n-Al\(_x\)Ga\(_{1-x}\)As cladding (\(N_d=3\times10^{17}\) cm⁻³), with \(\Delta E_g=1.247x\) eV split 67:33 between \(\Delta E_c\) and \(\Delta E_v\). The reference is the same structure with \(x=0\): identical doping, no offsets, so it is a homojunction. Both use \(B=10^{-10}\) cm³ s⁻¹, \(C_n=C_p=10^{-30}\) cm⁶ s⁻¹ and the SRH lifetime below, everywhere.

LEDs are compared at equal current, not equal voltage, so the slider sets the current density \(J\). The simulator picks the nearest computed bias point for each structure.

dhStep = 0.025
dhVmax = 1.5
dhOff = ({dEg: 1.247 * dhp.x, dEc: 0.67 * 1.247 * dhp.x, dEv: 0.33 * 1.247 * dhp.x})
// two voltage sweeps (heterostructure and reference), computed in chunks; a change of any input restarts them
dhSim = {
  const mk = (x) => new PNDiode({
    Na: 3e18, Nd: 3e17, tau_n: 10 ** dhp.ltau, tau_p: 10 ** dhp.ltau, mesh: [2e-7, 3e-5, 1.1, 1e-5],
    dh: {d: dhp.d * 1e-7, dEc: 0.67 * 1.247 * x, dEv: 0.33 * 1.247 * x, Nact: -(10 ** dhp.lNa)}
  });
  const keys = ["DH", "ref"], devs = {DH: mk(dhp.x), ref: mk(0)}, res = {DH: [], ref: []}, fail = {DH: null, ref: null}, u = {};
  yield Promises.delay(150, {devs, res, fail, done: false});
  for (const k of keys) {
    const r = devs[k].solve(devs[k].initialGuess(), 0, {maxit: 400});
    if (r.ok) u[k] = r.u; else fail[k] = 0;
  }
  const nV = Math.round(dhVmax / dhStep);
  for (let s = 1; s <= nV; s++) {
    for (const k of keys) {
      if (fail[k] !== null) continue;
      const r = devs[k].solve(u[k], s * dhStep);
      if (!r.ok) { fail[k] = s * dhStep; continue; }
      u[k] = r.u;
      res[k].push(devs[k].analyse(u[k], s * dhStep));
    }
    if (s % 5 === 0 && s < nV) yield {devs, res, fail, done: false};
  }
  yield {devs, res, fail, done: true};
}

What to look for, with the default structure (\(x=0.3\), \(d=100\) nm, \(N_a=10^{17}\) cm⁻³, \(\tau=100\) ns):

  1. Carrier confinement. In the band diagram, \(\Delta E_c\) stops the electrons at the p-cladding side and \(\Delta E_v\) stops the holes at the n-cladding side. Both carriers pile up in the active layer, and essentially all the light comes from it. In the carrier plot, at 30 A/cm² both \(n\) and \(p\) in the layer reach about \(4\times10^{17}\) cm⁻³. That is several hundred times the electron density injected into the p side of the reference, where the electrons spread over several µm.
  2. The IQE grows with current. Once \(n\approx p\gg N_a\) in the layer, the radiative rate goes as \(Bn^2\) while SRH goes as \(n/2\tau\), so \(\eta_\text{IQE}\approx 2B\tau n/(1+2B\tau n)\) rises with the injection. In the reference both rates are linear in the minority density and the IQE barely changes with \(J\). The heterostructure wins above a few A/cm².
  3. At low current the heterostructure is worse. With \(n\approx p\) the whole lightly doped layer behaves like the SCL of a homojunction, where SRH is strongest. Raise the active doping to \(10^{18}\) cm⁻³ and watch the low-current IQE recover.
  4. Thickness. A thinner layer gives a higher density at the same current, so the IQE rises earlier. Below about 20 nm the electron levels are quantised: this is a quantum well (Chapter 10), which this classical model does not describe.
  5. Barrier height. Lower \(x\) toward 0.05. With \(\Delta E_c\) only a few \(k_BT\), electrons spill into the p cladding and the recombination outside the layer grows.
  6. Lifetime. With \(\tau=10\) ns the heterostructure needs about 50 A/cm² to beat the reference. The quality of the active layer and of its interfaces matters as much as the band offsets.

The model leaves out the largest practical advantage of the heterostructure: the claddings are transparent to photons of energy \(E_g(\text{GaAs})\). In a homojunction most photons are generated a few µm deep in GaAs, which absorbs them (\(1/\alpha\approx1\) µm near the gap), whereas here they cross the claddings unattenuated. Other simplifications: abrupt offsets spread over one 2 nm mesh cell (no thermionic emission), no interface recombination, and the same masses, mobilities and permittivity in GaAs and AlGaAs.

## Key relations

Forward bias a pn⁺ junction and the barrier drops, so electrons from the n⁺ side are injected into the p side. They recombine
within a diffusion length of the junction and, in a direct-gap semiconductor, emit photons of energy close to the gap (slides
86–87):

$$
h\nu\approx E_g,\qquad \lambda\,[\mu\text{m}]\approx\frac{1.24}{E_g\,[\text{eV}]}.
$$

Under bias the single Fermi level splits into two **quasi-Fermi levels**, $E_{Fn}$ for electrons and $E_{Fp}$ for holes, whose
separation equals $eV$ (slide 87c). The emission colour is set by **bandgap engineering**: alloying binaries such as GaAs and
AlAs or GaN and InN shifts $E_g$ (slides 114–115). The alloy must keep the lattice constant of its substrate, otherwise
dislocations form and kill the radiative efficiency. Vegard's law interpolates the lattice constant,
$a(x)=xa_1+(1-x)a_2$, while the bandgap often bows, $E_g(x)=xE_1+(1-x)E_2-bx(1-x)$.

A **heterojunction** joins two different semiconductors (slides 93–96). Its band edges jump by $\Delta E_c$ and $\Delta E_v$. In a
**double heterostructure** (DH) a thin narrow-gap active layer sits between wide-gap cladding layers. The offsets trap the
injected carriers in the active layer, which raises the recombination rate. The cladding is also transparent to the emitted
photons and has a lower refractive index, so it guides the light. This is the planar waveguide of Chapter 2.

### Simulating a double heterostructure {#sec-dh-sim}

The drift–diffusion simulator of @sec-ddsim showed why a homojunction is a poor LED. The injected electrons spread over a
diffusion length of several µm, so their density stays low, and the radiative rate per electron, $BN_a$, is fixed by the
doping. The panel below adds the band offsets. A p-GaAs active layer of thickness $d$ sits between a p-Al$_x$Ga$_{1-x}$As
cladding ($N_a=3\times10^{18}$ cm⁻³) and an n-Al$_x$Ga$_{1-x}$As cladding ($N_d=3\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³), with
$\Delta E_g=1.247x$ eV split 67:33 between $\Delta E_c$ and $\Delta E_v$. The **reference** is the same structure with
$x=0$: identical doping, no offsets, so it is a homojunction. Both use $B=10^{-10}$ cm³ s⁻¹, $C_n=C_p=10^{-30}$ cm⁶ s⁻¹ and the
SRH lifetime below, everywhere.

LEDs are compared at equal **current**, not equal voltage, so the slider sets the current density $J$. The simulator picks the
nearest computed bias point for each structure.

      if (!r.ok) { fail[k] = s * dhStep; continue; }
      u[k] = r.u;
      res[k].push(devs[k].analyse(u[k], s * dhStep));

What to look for, with the default structure ($x=0.3$, $d=100$ nm, $N_a=10^{17}$ cm⁻³, $\tau=100$ ns):

1. **Carrier confinement.** In the band diagram, $\Delta E_c$ stops the electrons at the p-cladding side and $\Delta E_v$ stops the
   holes at the n-cladding side. Both carriers pile up in the active layer, and essentially all the light comes from it. In the
   carrier plot, at 30 A/cm² both $n$ and $p$ in the layer reach about $4\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³. That is several hundred times the
   electron density injected into the p side of the reference, where the electrons spread over several µm.
2. **The IQE grows with current.** Once $n\approx p\gg N_a$ in the layer, the radiative rate goes as $Bn^2$ while SRH goes as
   $n/2\tau$, so $\eta_\text{IQE}\approx 2B\tau n/(1+2B\tau n)$ rises with the injection. In the reference both rates are linear
   in the minority density and the IQE barely changes with $J$. The heterostructure wins above a few A/cm².
3. **At low current the heterostructure is worse.** With $n\approx p$ the whole lightly doped layer behaves like the SCL of a
   homojunction, where SRH is strongest. Raise the active doping to $10^{18}$ cm⁻³ and watch the low-current IQE recover.
4. **Thickness.** A thinner layer gives a higher density at the same current, so the IQE rises earlier. Below about 20 nm the
   electron levels are quantised: this is a quantum well (@sec-qw), which this classical model does not describe.
5. **Barrier height.** Lower $x$ toward 0.05. With $\Delta E_c$ only a few $k_BT$, electrons spill into the p cladding and the
   recombination outside the layer grows.
6. **Lifetime.** With $\tau=10$ ns the heterostructure needs about 50 A/cm² to beat the reference. The quality of the active
   layer and of its interfaces matters as much as the band offsets.

The model leaves out the largest practical advantage of the heterostructure: the claddings are **transparent** to photons of
energy $E_g(\text{GaAs})$. In a homojunction most photons are generated a few µm deep in GaAs, which absorbs them
($1/\alpha\approx1$ µm near the gap), whereas here they cross the claddings unattenuated. Other simplifications: abrupt offsets
spread over one 2 nm mesh cell (no thermionic emission), no interface recombination, and the same masses, mobilities and
permittivity in GaAs and AlGaAs.
