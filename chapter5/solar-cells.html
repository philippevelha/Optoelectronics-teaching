13.1 Key relations

A solar cell is a large-area pn junction operated in the fourth quadrant of the I–V plane (slide 122): it generates a current while the voltage across it is positive, so it delivers power \(P=-IV\) to a load. With the convention of slide 123,

\[ I=-I_{ph}+I_o\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{eV}{\eta k_BT}\right)-1\right],\qquad I_{ph}=K\,I_\text{light}, \]

where \(K\) is a device constant and \(I_\text{light}\) the light intensity (slide 124). Two limits characterise the cell:

\[ I_{sc}=-I_{ph},\qquad V_{oc}=\frac{\eta k_BT}{e}\ln\!\left(\frac{I_{ph}}{I_o}+1\right). \]

With a load \(R\) the operating point is the intersection of the cell curve with the load line \(I=-V/R\) (slide 125). The power is the area of the rectangle \(|IV|\); it is maximum at the maximum power point \((V_m,I_m)\), and the fill factor measures how “square” the curve is (slide 126):

\[ \text{FF}=\frac{I_mV_m}{I_{sc}V_{oc}},\qquad \text{efficiency}=\frac{I_mV_m}{P_\text{in}}=\frac{\text{FF}\,I_{sc}V_{oc}}{I_\text{light}A}. \]

Real cells have a series resistance \(R_s\) (finger electrodes, thin emitter, contacts) and a shunt resistance \(R_p\) (leakage at edges and defects) (slides 127–128):

\[ I=-I_{ph}+I_o\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{e(V-IR_s)}{\eta k_BT}\right)-1\right]+\frac{V-IR_s}{R_p}. \]

\(R_s\) tilts the curve near \(V_{oc}\) and reduces the FF and eventually \(I_{sc}\) (slide 129); a small \(R_p\) tilts it near \(I_{sc}\).

scOut = {
  const Iph = sc.Jsc * 1e-3 * sc.A * sc.G / 1000, Io = 10 ** sc.lJo * sc.A, Rs = sc.Rs / sc.A, Rp = 10 ** sc.lRp / sc.A;

}

Every cell reaches only 50–80 % of its band gap in voltage. Why not 100 %? Because a cell at 300 K in equilibrium with its surroundings must emit as much as it absorbs (detailed balance): the Shockley–Queisser analysis of Chapter 15 turns this into a fundamental efficiency limit of about 31–33 % for a single junction.

TipGoing further
  • Temperature coefficient. \(V_{oc}\) falls by about 2 mV/K in Si because \(I_o\propto n_i^2\propto T^3e^{-E_g/k_BT}\). Derive \(dV_{oc}/dT\approx-(E_g/e-V_{oc}+3k_BT/e)/T\) and compute the loss of a module at 65 °C.
  • Light trapping. The inverted pyramids of slide 131 make light travel obliquely and reflect totally at the back: the optical path is enhanced up to \(4n^2\approx50\) times (Yablonovitch limit). Combine with α(λ) of Si (Chapter 3) to compute the absorbed fraction at 1000 nm for a 150 µm wafer with and without trapping.
  • Current matching in tandems. In a series-connected tandem (slide 132) the same current flows through both cells; the top cell must be thin enough or have the right gap to share photons equally. Use the SQ model of Chapter 15 to find the optimum pair of gaps.
  • Partial shading. In a module of 60 cells in series, one shaded cell is reverse biased by the others. Why do modules have bypass diodes, and what is a “hot spot”?
  • Maximum-power-point tracking. The optimum load changes with intensity and temperature. Write a perturb-and-observe algorithm that finds the MPP on the curves of the panel.

## Key relations

the voltage across it is positive, so it **delivers** power $P=-IV$ to a load. With the convention of slide 123,

$$

$$

where $K$ is a device constant and $I_\text{light}$ the light intensity (slide 124). Two limits characterise the cell:

$$
I_{sc}=-I_{ph},\qquad V_{oc}=\frac{\eta k_BT}{e}\ln\!\left(\frac{I_{ph}}{I_o}+1\right).
$$

With a load $R$ the operating point is the intersection of the cell curve with the load line $I=-V/R$ (slide 125). The power is the
area of the rectangle $|IV|$; it is maximum at the **maximum power point** $(V_m,I_m)$, and the **fill factor** measures how
"square" the curve is (slide 126):

$$

$$

$$

$R_s$ tilts the curve near $V_{oc}$ and reduces the FF and eventually $I_{sc}$ (slide 129); a small $R_p$ tilts it near $I_{sc}$.

Every cell reaches only 50–80 % of its band gap in voltage. Why not 100 %? Because a cell at 300 K in equilibrium with its surroundings
must emit as much as it absorbs (detailed balance): the Shockley–Queisser analysis of @sec-sq turns this into a fundamental efficiency
limit of about 31–33 % for a single junction.

## Going further
- **Temperature coefficient.** $V_{oc}$ falls by about 2 mV/K in Si because $I_o\propto n_i^2\propto T^3e^{-E_g/k_BT}$. Derive
  $dV_{oc}/dT\approx-(E_g/e-V_{oc}+3k_BT/e)/T$ and compute the loss of a module at 65 °C.
- **Light trapping.** The inverted pyramids of slide 131 make light travel obliquely and reflect totally at the back: the optical path
  is enhanced up to $4n^2\approx50$ times (Yablonovitch limit). Combine with α(λ) of Si (@sec-absorption) to compute the absorbed fraction
  at 1000 nm for a 150 µm wafer with and without trapping.
- **Current matching in tandems.** In a series-connected tandem (slide 132) the same current flows through both cells; the top cell must
  be thin enough or have the right gap to share photons equally. Use the SQ model of @sec-sq to find the optimum pair of gaps.
- **Partial shading.** In a module of 60 cells in series, one shaded cell is reverse biased by the others. Why do modules have bypass
  diodes, and what is a "hot spot"?
- **Maximum-power-point tracking.** The optimum load changes with intensity and temperature. Write a perturb-and-observe algorithm that
  finds the MPP on the curves of the panel.
