8.1 Key relations

A photoconductor is just a slab of semiconductor of length \(\ell\), width \(w\) and thickness \(d\) with two ohmic contacts (slide 70). Light creates excess carriers \(\Delta n=\Delta p\) that raise the conductivity. Under a steady photon flux the generation rate per unit volume is \(g_{ph}=\eta_iI\lambda/(hcd)\) for an intensity \(I\) absorbed in the thickness \(d\), and the excess density settles at \(\Delta n=g_{ph}\tau\), with τ the recombination lifetime (slide 72):

\[ \Delta\sigma=e\Delta n(\mu_e+\mu_h)=\frac{e\eta_iI\lambda\tau(\mu_e+\mu_h)}{hcd},\qquad J_{ph}=\Delta\sigma\frac{V}{\ell}=\Delta\sigma E . \]

The photoconductive gain compares the rate of electrons flowing in the external circuit with the rate of pair generation (slides 73–74):

\[ G=\frac{\tau(\mu_e+\mu_h)E}{\ell}=\frac{\tau}{t_e}+\frac{\tau}{t_h}=\frac{\tau}{t_e}\left(1+\frac{\mu_h}{\mu_e}\right),\qquad t_e=\frac{\ell}{\mu_eE},\ t_h=\frac{\ell}{\mu_hE}. \]

\(G\) can be much larger than 1. The reason is charge neutrality with ohmic contacts (slide 71): while a slow hole (or a hole trapped at a defect) is still in the slab, every electron that leaves at the anode is replaced by a new one injected at the cathode. One absorbed photon therefore lets \(\tau/t_e\) electrons circulate. The catch is speed: the photocurrent rises and decays with the lifetime τ, so the bandwidth is about \(1/2\pi\tau\) and

\[ G\times B\approx\frac{\tau}{t_e}\cdot\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}=\frac{1}{2\pi t_e}=\frac{\mu_eV}{2\pi\ell^2} \]

is independent of τ: gain is bought with bandwidth. Photoconductors (PbS, PbSe, InSb, HgCdTe, CdS light-dependent resistors) are used where a simple, sensitive, slow detector is enough: radiation thermometers, flame monitors, gas analysers, spectrometers in the 1–6 µm range (slides 69, 146).

8.4 Live panel: chopper and lock-in amplifier (slides 147–148)

Photoconductive IR detectors are read with a chopped beam and a lock-in amplifier (slide 148). The chopper modulates the light at \(f_c\); the lock-in multiplies the detector signal by a reference square wave synchronised with the chopper and averages the product with a time constant \(T_c\). Noise at other frequencies averages to zero; only the in-phase component at \(f_c\) survives. The equivalent noise bandwidth is about \(1/(4T_c)\), which can be a fraction of a hertz.

Show the Python 
import {rng} from "./det_sim.js"
viewof li = Inputs.form({
  snr: Inputs.range([-40, 10], {value: -20, step: 1, label: "input SNR (signal amplitude / noise rms, dB)"}),
  fc: Inputs.range([50, 2000], {value: 300, step: 10, label: "chopper frequency f_c (Hz)"}),
  Tc: Inputs.range([1, 3000], {value: 300, step: 1, label: "lock-in time constant T_c (ms)"}),
  flick: Inputs.range([0, 20], {value: 5, step: 0.5, label: "1/f (drift) noise amplitude (× signal)"}),
  seed: Inputs.range([1, 50], {value: 7, step: 1, label: "noise seed"})
})
Show the Python 
liOut = {
  const r = rng(li.seed), fs = 20000, Ttot = Math.max(5 * li.Tc / 1000, 0.5), N = Math.min(Math.round(fs * Ttot), 200000);
  const A = 1, sigma = A / 10 ** (li.snr / 20), raw = [], out = [];
  let drift = 0, y = 0, yq = 0;
  const alphaLP = 1 / (li.Tc / 1000 * fs);
  for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
    const t = i / fs, ref = Math.sin(2 * Math.PI * li.fc * t) >= 0 ? 1 : -1, light = ref > 0 ? A : 0;
    drift += (r.gauss() * 0.02 - drift * 0.0005);                      // slow random walk (1/f-like)
    const v = light + sigma * r.gauss() + li.flick * A * drift;
    y += alphaLP * (v * ref - y);                                      // phase-sensitive detection + RC low-pass
    if (i % Math.max(1, Math.floor(N / 1500)) === 0) { raw.push({t, v}); out.push({t, y: 2 * y}); }
  }
  return {raw, out, final: 2 * y};
}
Plot.plot({
  width: Math.min(width, 1050), height: 200, x: {label: "time (s)"}, y: {label: "detector signal", grid: true},
  marks: [Plot.line(liOut.raw, {x: "t", y: "v", stroke: "#c3c2b7", strokeWidth: 0.8})]
})
Show the Python 
Plot.plot({
  width: Math.min(width, 1050), height: 200, x: {label: "time (s)"}, y: {label: "lock-in output", grid: true, domain: [-0.5, 1.5]},
  marks: [Plot.line(liOut.out, {x: "t", y: "y", stroke: "#2a78d6", strokeWidth: 2}), Plot.ruleY([1], {stroke: "#e34948", strokeDasharray: "4,3"})]
})
Show the Python 
html`<div class="result-box">true signal amplitude 1 · lock-in output after ${(liOut.raw.at(-1).t).toFixed(2)} s: <b>${liOut.final.toFixed(3)}</b> ·
noise bandwidth ≈ 1/(4T<sub>c</sub>) = ${(1000 / (4 * li.Tc)).toPrecision(3)} Hz (input noise spread over 10 kHz)</div>`

At −20 dB the signal is invisible in the raw trace, yet the lock-in recovers it within a few percent after a few time constants. The drift (1/f) noise, which would ruin a DC measurement, is rejected because the chopper moves the signal to \(f_c\), away from low frequencies. The price is time: halving the noise requires four times longer averaging.

TipGoing further
  • Generation–recombination noise. The random generation and recombination of carriers gives a current noise \(\langle i^2\rangle=4eIGB/(1+(2\pi f\tau)^2)\), twice the shot noise of a photodiode with the same gain. Compare the NEP of a PbS photoconductor with a cooled InAs photodiode at 2.4 µm.
  • Blocking contacts. With contacts that do not inject carriers (Schottky or pn), a carrier that leaves is not replaced and \(G\le1\). This is the photodiode limit; ohmic contacts are what make photoconductive gain possible.
  • Persistent photoconductivity and traps. If holes are trapped for a long time, τ (and \(G\)) becomes huge but the detector “remembers” past illumination. This is the mechanism behind many record responsivities claimed for nanomaterial detectors: judge them by \(G\times B\) and by the noise, not by \(\mathcal R\) alone.
  • Quantum-well infrared photodetectors (QWIPs) are photoconductors that use intersubband transitions in GaAs/AlGaAs wells to detect 8–12 µm thermal radiation. Their gain \(G=\tau_c/\tau_{tr}\) is usually below 1. Why?

At −20 dB the signal is invisible in the raw trace, yet the lock-in recovers it within a few percent after a few time constants.
The drift (1/f) noise, which would ruin a DC measurement, is rejected because the chopper moves the signal to $f_c$, away from
low frequencies. The price is time: halving the noise requires four times longer averaging.

## Going further
- **Generation–recombination noise.** The random generation *and* recombination of carriers gives a current noise
  $\langle i^2\rangle=4eIGB/(1+(2\pi f\tau)^2)$, twice the shot noise of a photodiode with the same gain. Compare the NEP of a PbS
  photoconductor with a cooled InAs photodiode at 2.4 µm.
- **Blocking contacts.** With contacts that do not inject carriers (Schottky or pn), a carrier that leaves is not replaced and
  $G\le1$. This is the photodiode limit; ohmic contacts are what make photoconductive gain possible.
- **Persistent photoconductivity and traps.** If holes are trapped for a long time, τ (and $G$) becomes huge but the detector
  "remembers" past illumination. This is the mechanism behind many record responsivities claimed for nanomaterial detectors: judge
  them by $G\times B$ and by the noise, not by $\mathcal R$ alone.
- **Quantum-well infrared photodetectors (QWIPs)** are photoconductors that use intersubband transitions in GaAs/AlGaAs wells to
  detect 8–12 µm thermal radiation. Their gain $G=\tau_c/\tau_{tr}$ is usually *below* 1. Why?
:::
