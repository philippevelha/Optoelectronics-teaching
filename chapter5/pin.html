5.1 Key relations

A pn junction has a narrow depletion region whose width depends on the bias (Ch. 3), so most light is absorbed in the neutral regions and collected slowly by diffusion. The pin photodiode inserts an intrinsic (lightly doped) layer of width \(W\) between p⁺ and n⁺ (slide 29). The depletion region is the whole i-layer, nearly independent of the bias, with a uniform field (slide 30):

\[ E=E_o+\frac{V_r}{W}\approx\frac{V_r}{W},\qquad C_\text{dep}=\frac{\varepsilon_o\varepsilon_rA}{W},\qquad t_\text{drift}=\frac{W}{v_d}. \]

The drift velocity saturates at high field, about \(10^5\) m/s for electrons in Si above \(\sim3\times10^6\) V/m (slide 31), so the transit time cannot be reduced indefinitely by raising \(V_r\). Carriers generated in the p⁺ layer outside the field must diffuse to the i-layer; in a time \(t\) they diffuse on average (slide 32)

\[ \ell=\sqrt{2D_et}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_\text{diff}=\frac{\ell^2}{2D_e}, \]

which is much slower than drift and produces the slow diffusion tail of the pulse response (Chapter 9).

The pin therefore embodies the central trade-off of photodetection: a wide \(W\) absorbs more light (high \(\mathcal R\), Chapter 4) and has a small capacitance (small \(RC\)), but a long transit time. A narrow \(W\) is fast in transit but absorbs little and has a large capacitance. For a given load there is an optimum width.

5.4 Live panel: designing a fast Si pin (transit time versus RC)

The bandwidth combines the transit-time response of Chapter 2 (generation \(e^{-\alpha x}\) in the i-layer, electrons and holes at their field-dependent velocities) with the \(RC\) low-pass of the junction capacitance and the load (Chapter 9): \(|H(f)|=|H_\text{transit}(f)|\,/\,|1+j2\pi fRC|\).

  Vr: Inputs.range([1, 200], {value: 20, step: 1, label: "reverse bias V_r (V)"}),
  D: Inputs.range([10, 3000], {value: 300, step: 10, label: "active diameter (µm)"}),

Show the Python 
pinOut = {
  const a = alpha("Si", pn2.wl * 1e-9), A = Math.PI * (pn2.D * 1e-6 / 2) ** 2, T = pn2.ar ? 1 : fresnelT(3.6);
  const bw = (Wum) => {
    const W = Wum * 1e-6, E = pn2.Vr / W, ve = driftVelocity(E, "e"), vh = driftVelocity(E, "h");
    const C = eps0 * 11.9 * A / W, fRC = 1 / (2 * Math.PI * pn2.RL * C);
    const ftr0 = 0.45 * Math.min(ve, vh) / W, fmax = 3 * Math.min(ftr0 * 2, fRC * 2);
    let f3 = NaN, ftr = NaN;
    for (let i = 1; i <= 600; i++) { const f = fmax * i / 600, Ht = transitMag(f, W, a, ve, vh, 60);
      if (isNaN(ftr) && Ht < Math.SQRT1_2) ftr = f;
      if (Ht / Math.hypot(1, f / fRC) < Math.SQRT1_2) { f3 = f; break; } }
    return {W: Wum, f3, fRC, ftr, ve, vh, C, E, R: responsivity(T, pn2.wl * 1e-9) * (1 - Math.exp(-a * W))};
  };
  const cur = bw(pn2.W), sweep = d3.range(0, 61).map(i => bw(0.5 * 200 ** (i / 60)));
  const best = sweep.reduce((b, s) => (s.f3 > b.f3 ? s : b), sweep[0]);
  const bestRB = sweep.reduce((b, s) => (s.f3 * s.R > b.f3 * b.R ? s : b), sweep[0]);
  return {cur, sweep, best, bestRB, a};
}

Things to try: with a 300 µm detector on 50 Ω the RC limit dominates below about 9 µm and the transit time above; the optimum, near 9 µm, gives 2.6 GHz but only 0.26 A/W at 850 nm. A 1 mm detector is optimal near 22 µm and 0.6 GHz. Shrink the diameter to 30 µm (a fiber-coupled datacom detector): the optimum moves to about 1 µm and 30 GHz, but Si then absorbs less than 5 % at 850 nm. That is why 25 Gb/s receivers at 850 nm use GaAs (α 20 times larger) and why 1310/1550 nm receivers use InGaAs layers about 1 µm thick. Raise \(V_r\) at small \(W\): nothing changes once the velocities saturate.

TipGoing further
  • The optimum width analytically. With a single carrier at saturation, \(f_\text{tr}\approx0.45v_s/W\) and \(f_{RC}=W/(2\pi R\varepsilon A)\). Setting \(f_\text{tr}=f_{RC}\) gives \(W_\text{opt}=\sqrt{0.9\pi R\varepsilon Av_s}\) and a maximum bandwidth \(\propto1/\sqrt{A}\). Check this against the panel.
  • Velocity overshoot and hot carriers in InGaAs/InP let electrons cross thin layers at 3–5×10⁵ m/s, faster than the equilibrium saturation velocity. Combine with the UTC structure of Chapter 2.
  • Inductive peaking. A small series inductance (bond wire) can extend the RC bandwidth by up to 70 % at the cost of ringing. Simulate an RLC low-pass with \(L=0.5\) nH, \(C=0.3\) pF, \(R=50\) Ω.
  • Silicon for 1550 nm? Ge-on-Si waveguide photodiodes are now standard in silicon photonics transceivers: Ge grown on Si absorbs to about 1.6 µm thanks to tensile strain, which lowers its direct gap.

near 9 µm, gives 2.6 GHz but only 0.26 A/W at 850 nm. A 1 mm detector is optimal near 22 µm and 0.6 GHz. Shrink the diameter to

use InGaAs layers about 1 µm thick. Raise $V_r$ at small $W$: nothing changes once the velocities saturate.

## Going further
- **The optimum width analytically.** With a single carrier at saturation, $f_\text{tr}\approx0.45v_s/W$ and $f_{RC}=W/(2\pi R\varepsilon A)$.
  Setting $f_\text{tr}=f_{RC}$ gives $W_\text{opt}=\sqrt{0.9\pi R\varepsilon Av_s}$ and a maximum bandwidth $\propto1/\sqrt{A}$. Check this
  against the panel.
- **Velocity overshoot and hot carriers** in InGaAs/InP let electrons cross thin layers at 3–5×10⁵ m/s, faster than the
  equilibrium saturation velocity. Combine with the UTC structure of @sec-ramo.
- **Inductive peaking.** A small series inductance (bond wire) can extend the RC bandwidth by up to 70 % at the cost of ringing.
  Simulate an RLC low-pass with $L=0.5$ nH, $C=0.3$ pF, $R=50$ Ω.
- **Silicon for 1550 nm?** Ge-on-Si waveguide photodiodes are now standard in silicon photonics transceivers: Ge grown on Si
  absorbs to about 1.6 µm thanks to tensile strain, which lowers its direct gap.
:::
