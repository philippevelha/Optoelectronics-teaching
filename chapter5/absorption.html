3.1 Key relations

Light entering a semiconductor is absorbed exponentially (Beer–Lambert, slide 16):

\[ I(x)=I_o\,e^{-\alpha x},\qquad \delta=\frac1\alpha\ \text{(penetration depth)},\qquad \text{fraction absorbed in a thickness }d:\ 1-e^{-\alpha d}. \]

Band-to-band absorption needs \(h\nu\ge E_g\), which defines the cut-off wavelength (slide 18)

\[ \lambda_g\,[\mu\text{m}]\approx\frac{1.24}{E_g\,[\text{eV}]} . \]

Momentum must also be conserved. A photon carries almost no momentum (\(\hbar k_\text{photon}\ll\hbar\pi/a\)), so in a direct gap semiconductor (GaAs, InP, InGaAs) the transition is vertical in \(k\) and α rises steeply above \(E_g\), roughly as \(\sqrt{h\nu-E_g}\), to \(10^6\)–\(10^7\) m⁻¹ (slide 17). In an indirect gap semiconductor (Si, Ge) the conduction-band minimum is at a different \(k\): a phonon of wavevector \(K=k_{CB}-k_{VB}\) must be absorbed or emitted (slide 19), so \(h\nu=E_g\pm h\vartheta\) with \(h\vartheta<0.1\) eV. This second-order process is weak: α of Si is only \(10^3\)–\(10^5\) m⁻¹ over the near infrared, and a Si detector for 900 nm must be tens of µm thick. Ge is indirect (\(E_g=0.66\) eV) but has a direct gap at 0.80 eV just above it: its absorption jumps by two orders of magnitude below 1.55 µm (slide 16), which is why Ge photodiodes work at 1550 nm.

3.4 Live panel: the absorption edge moves with temperature (slide 35)

The band gap shrinks as the lattice expands and phonons multiply (Varshni, \(E_g(T)=E_{g0}-aT^2/(T+b)\)). The absorption edge, and hence the long-wavelength cut-off of the responsivity, moves to longer wavelengths when the detector warms up. Slide 35 shows it for an InAs photodiode. The model below is a generic direct-gap edge, \(\alpha=A\sqrt{h\nu-E_g}\) joined to an exponential (Urbach) tail of width \(E_U\).

Show the Python 
vtOut = {
  const [Eg0, a, b, A] = VARSHNI[vt.mat], Eg = T => Eg0 - a * T * T / (T + b), EU = vt.EU / 1000;
  const alphaE = (E, g) => { const E1 = g + EU / 2; return E > E1 ? A * Math.sqrt(E - g) : A * Math.sqrt(EU / 2) * Math.exp((E - E1) / EU); };
  const g1 = Eg(vt.T1), g2 = Eg(vt.T2), lg = 1.23984 / Math.min(g1, g2), pts = [];
  for (let i = 0; i <= 500; i++) {
    const wl = 0.6 * lg + 0.6 * lg * i / 500, E = 1.23984 / wl;
    for (const [T, g] of [[vt.T1, g1], [vt.T2, g2]]) {
      const al = alphaE(E, g); pts.push({wl, eta: (1 - Math.exp(-al * vt.d * 1e-6)) , R: (1 - Math.exp(-al * vt.d * 1e-6)) * wl / 1.23984, T: `T = ${T} K`});
    }
  }
  return {pts, g1, g2};
}

})

Two lessons. First, the long-wavelength edge of a detector is not a sharp line: the Urbach tail and a thick absorber push it beyond \(\lambda_g\), which is how InGaAs photodiodes still respond at 1.65–1.7 µm. Second, a detector operated near its cut-off is a thermometer: at 1.65 µm the responsivity of InGaAs changes by several % per kelvin. Wavelength-critical measurements near the edge need temperature control.

TipGoing further
  • Indirect absorption has two branches. Phonon absorption gives \(\alpha_a\propto(h\nu-E_g+h\vartheta)^2/(e^{h\vartheta/k_BT}-1)\) and phonon emission \(\alpha_e\propto(h\nu-E_g-h\vartheta)^2/(1-e^{-h\vartheta/k_BT})\). Plot \(\sqrt\alpha\) versus \(h\nu\) for Si near 1.1 µm (“Macfarlane plot”) and extract \(E_g\) and the phonon energy (≈ 58 meV). Why does Si absorb less at 1.1 µm when it is cooled?
  • Free-carrier absorption (\(\alpha_{fc}\propto n\lambda^2\)) competes in heavily doped contact layers and limits the long-wavelength transparency of substrates (Ch. 4, the silicon laser problem).
  • Two-photon absorption lets a Si detector respond to intense 1.55 µm pulses (\(\alpha=\beta I\), \(\beta\approx1\) cm/GW): it is used for autocorrelators of femtosecond pulses. What intensity gives the same α as one-photon absorption at 1.1 µm?
  • Kramers–Kronig. Absorption and refractive index are linked (Ch. 1). The strong band-edge absorption of InGaAs causes the dispersion of \(n\) that sets the group index of laser cavities (Ch. 4).
NoteComplements
  • M. A. Green, “Self-consistent optical parameters of intrinsic silicon at 300 K”, Sol. Energy Mater. Sol. Cells 92, 1305 (2008).
  • I. Vurgaftman, J. R. Meyer, L. R. Ram-Mohan, “Band parameters for III–V compound semiconductors”, J. Appl. Phys. 89, 5815 (2001): Varshni parameters.
  • The refractive-index database https://refractiveindex.info gives tabulated \(n\) and \(k\) (\(\alpha=4\pi k/\lambda\)) for all these materials.

- **Indirect absorption has two branches.** Phonon absorption gives $\alpha_a\propto(h\nu-E_g+h\vartheta)^2/(e^{h\vartheta/k_BT}-1)$ and
  phonon emission $\alpha_e\propto(h\nu-E_g-h\vartheta)^2/(1-e^{-h\vartheta/k_BT})$. Plot $\sqrt\alpha$ versus $h\nu$ for Si near 1.1 µm
  ("Macfarlane plot") and extract $E_g$ and the phonon energy (≈ 58 meV). Why does Si absorb less at 1.1 µm when it is cooled?
- **Free-carrier absorption** ($\alpha_{fc}\propto n\lambda^2$) competes in heavily doped contact layers and limits the long-wavelength
  transparency of substrates (Ch. 4, the silicon laser problem).
- **Two-photon absorption** lets a Si detector respond to intense 1.55 µm pulses ($\alpha=\beta I$, $\beta\approx1$ cm/GW): it is used
  for autocorrelators of femtosecond pulses. What intensity gives the same α as one-photon absorption at 1.1 µm?
- **Kramers–Kronig.** Absorption and refractive index are linked (Ch. 1). The strong band-edge absorption of InGaAs causes the
  dispersion of $n$ that sets the group index of laser cavities (Ch. 4).
:::
