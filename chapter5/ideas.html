16.2 Mini-projects

For students who want to go further. Each reuses detlib.py and can be done in one to three weeks.

# project skills difficulty
1 Characterise a photodiode: measure dark I–V (extract \(I_o\), η), responsivity versus λ with a monochromator or LEDs, linearity with a load resistor (load line!), and the pulse response with a fast LED driver; compare with the models of Chapter 4 and Chapter 9 experiment, fitting ★
2 Design a 25 Gb/s 850 nm receiver: choose GaAs or Si, the absorber thickness and area, compute the transit/RC bandwidth and the sensitivity with a realistic TIA (include \(e_nC\) noise) device design, noise ★★
3 Drift–diffusion photodiode simulator: solve the 1D continuity equations (finite differences) for a pn photodiode under a light pulse; reproduce the drift + diffusion pulse of slide 85 and the internal QE of slide 145 numerics, semiconductor physics ★★★
4 APD Monte Carlo: simulate random impact ionisation chains in a uniform field with given \(\alpha_e\), \(\alpha_h\); build the gain distribution and compare \(F\) with McIntyre; add a dead space stochastic simulation ★★
5 Photon transfer of a webcam or phone camera: record flat fields with RAW output (rawpy), build the photon transfer curve, extract the conversion gain, read noise, full well and dark current versus exposure and temperature experiment, statistics ★★
6 Solar panel I–V tracer: measure a small panel with an electronic load (or a capacitor charging), extract \(I_{sc}\), \(V_{oc}\), FF, \(R_s\), \(R_p\) by fitting the one-diode model, follow the MPP during a day; compare with pvlib irradiance models experiment, fitting ★
7 Lock-in amplifier in software: digitise a chopped photodiode signal with a sound card, implement the lock-in in Python and measure a signal 40 dB below the noise signal processing ★★
8 Single-photon statistics: with a SPAD module (or a simulated one with dead time and after-pulsing), measure the photon-number distribution of an attenuated laser and of an LED; compare with Poisson and Bose–Einstein quantum optics ★★★

16.4 Improvements to the course

  1. One trade-off per device. Each detector of the chapter trades something for something: thickness for speed (pin), gain for noise (APD), gain for bandwidth (photoconductor, phototransistor), fill factor for speed (MSM), voltage for current (solar cell band gap). Framing the chapter this way, with the live panels of Chapter 5, Chapter 11 and Chapter 8, makes it coherent.
  2. “Predict, then compute.” “Does doubling the light double V_oc?”, “Does raising V_r make a pin faster?”, “Does a higher APD gain always improve the SNR?”, “Is a detector with R = 100 A/W better than one with R = 0.5 A/W?”
  3. Close the link budget. The receiver chapter (Chapter 14) ties Ch. 2 (BER, attenuation, dispersion), Ch. 4 (EDFA noise) and Ch. 5 (photodiode noise) into a single calculation: a good capstone exercise.
  4. Lab connection. A Si photodiode, a few LEDs of different colours, a load resistor box, an oscilloscope and a small solar panel cover responsivity, the load line, the RC response and the fill factor in one afternoon.
  5. Modern content. Possible updates: Ge-on-Si waveguide photodiodes for silicon photonics, SPAD arrays for lidar, SNSPDs, backside-illuminated stacked CMOS sensors, perovskite and perovskite/Si tandem solar cells, coherent receivers.

16.5 Notes for the instructor: checks on the slides

While recomputing the examples, everything matched the slides (QE and responsivity examples, pin transit and diffusion times, steady-state photocurrent with and without AR coating, Table 5.7, APD responsivities, Chynoweth example, ideal-detector and NEP examples, receiver SNR, APD noise example, solar-cell load line, \(V_{oc}\) versus intensity, efficiency table), except these points:

  • Slide numbering: the companion uses the 152 slides of chapter5_ Photodetectors.ppt.
  • Slide 20: the column header “\(\lambda_g\) (eV)” should read “\(\lambda_g\) (µm)” (Chapter 3).
  • Slide 16: the In₀.₅₃Ga₀.₄₇As curve gives about \(2\times10^5\) m⁻¹ at 1.55 µm, three times below literature values (\(\approx7\times10^5\) m⁻¹); the companion uses the literature values for InGaAs.
  • Slide 40: “at this field the drift velocity saturates at \(10^5\) m s⁻¹”: at \(3\times10^6\) V/m the Caughey–Thomas model gives \(0.89\times10^5\) m/s (0.22 ns instead of 0.2 ns); the conclusion is unchanged (Chapter 5).
  • Slide 49: the dark current of the GaP pin is printed “1 nm” (1 nA); the footnote letters are shuffled (“ᶜFGAP71” should be “ᵃ”).
  • Slide 56: “\(R=MR=(12)/(0.75)\)” should be \((12)(0.75)=9.0\) A/W (Chapter 6).
  • Slide 59 (Table 5.3): with unrounded coefficients \(M=58.7\) at \(4.30\times10^5\) V/cm and 644 at \(4.38\times10^5\) V/cm (slide: 57.2 and 647). The difference comes from rounding \(k\) to three digits and illustrates how sensitive \(M\) is near breakdown.
  • Slide 97: the GaP Schottky NEP (\(5.4\times10^{-15}\) W Hz⁻¹ᐟ²) is three times lower than the shot-noise value for the quoted \(I_d=10\) pA; the other entries agree with \(\sqrt{2eI_d}/\mathcal R\) within 20 % (Chapter 10).
  • Slide 102: “for a bandwidth of 1 Hz, NEP = \(2hc/\lambda\)” is correct numerically, but the NEP of an ideal (quantum-noise-limited) detector grows as \(\sqrt B\); NEP is bandwidth-independent only when the noise does not depend on the signal (Chapter 10, warning box).
  • Slide 105: “Noise from load: \(4k_BT/R_L\) = 1.29 nA” should read \([4k_BTB/R_L]^{1/2}=1.29\) nA.
  • Slides 106–107: the thermal noise of the load is neglected. With a 50 Ω load at 700 MHz it is 0.48 µA rms, and about 9 times more optical power (0.2 µW) is needed for SNR = 10 (Chapter 11). Worth a sentence on the slide, since it is the reason APDs exist.
  • Slides 133–135: the I–V curve of the 16 Ω example corresponds to an ideality factor η ≈ 2; the optimum load is 15.5 Ω and the true FF 0.727 (the slide’s 0.72 assumes the 16 Ω point is the MPP, which is very nearly true) (Chapter 13).
  • Slide 149: NEP is printed “W Hz” (W Hz⁻¹ᐟ²). Slide 150: “0.95 W/A” should be A/W.
  • Slide 130: the a-Si:H efficiency recomputed from \(V_{oc}J_{sc}\)FF is 9.9 % (slide 10.1 %, rounding in the source table).
  • Missing material added as “Going further”: transit-time frequency response and the transit/RC optimum (Chapter 2, Chapter 5), McIntyre excess noise and the optimum APD gain (Chapter 11), internal photoemission and MSM capacitance (Chapter 7), gain–bandwidth of photoconductors and phototransistors, lock-in detection, photon transfer curve, receiver sensitivity and BER (Chapter 14), and the Shockley–Queisser limit (Chapter 15).

## Python toolbox

All worked examples run with the scientific core only. The packages below extend them.

| Package | Install | What it is good for | Sections |
