4.1 Key relations

The external quantum efficiency counts collected electrons per incident photon, and the responsivity converts it to amperes per watt (slides 21–22):

\[ \eta_e=\frac{I_{ph}/e}{P_o/h\nu},\qquad \mathcal R=\frac{I_{ph}}{P_o}=\eta_e\frac{e}{h\nu}=\eta_e\frac{e\lambda}{hc}=\eta_e\,\frac{\lambda\,[\mu\text m]}{1.24}\ \text{A/W}. \]

An ideal photodiode (\(\eta_e=1\)) has \(\mathcal R\) growing linearly with λ up to the cut-off \(\lambda_g\), then falling to zero (slide 24). Real photodiodes lose photons by reflection at the surface (transmission \(T\)), by absorption in regions from which carriers are not collected, and by transmission through the device. With the internal quantum efficiency \(\eta_i\) (pairs per absorbed photon, slide 28) and the useful (collecting) region between \(x_1=\ell_p-L_e\) and \(x_2=\ell_p+W+L_h\) (slides 139–144):

\[ I_{ph}=\frac{e\eta_iTP_o}{h\nu}\left[e^{-\alpha x_1}-e^{-\alpha x_2}\right], \qquad \text{pin with thin p}^+:\ I_{ph}\approx\frac{e\eta_iTP_o}{h\nu}\left[1-e^{-\alpha W}\right]. \]

At short λ (large α) light is absorbed in the p⁺ layer and near the surface, where carriers recombine; at long λ (small α) it passes through the collecting region. The responsivity peaks in between. Because \(\eta_e=\mathcal Rhc/e\lambda\), the QE is maximum where \(d(\mathcal R/\lambda)/d\lambda=0\), i.e. where \(d\mathcal R/d\lambda=\mathcal R/\lambda\): the tangent to the \(\mathcal R(\lambda)\) curve passes through the origin (slide 26). That point is not the peak of \(\mathcal R\).

Show the Python 
rsOut = {
  const m = MATERIALS[rs.mat], T = rs.ar.startsWith("perfect") ? 1 : fresnelT(m.n), lg = 1.23984 / m.Eg, pts = [], ideal = [];
  let best = {eta: 0}, peak = {R: 0};
  for (let i = 0; i <= 600; i++) {
    const wl = (0.3 + (lg * 1.05 - 0.3) * i / 600) * 1e-6, a = alpha(rs.mat, wl);
    const R = structureR(wl, a, rs.lp * 1e-6, rs.W * 1e-6, rs.Le * 1e-6, rs.Lh * 1e-6, T, rs.etai);
    pts.push({wl: wl * 1e9, R, c: "this structure"}); ideal.push({wl: wl * 1e9, R: wl * 1e6 <= lg ? responsivity(1, wl) : 0, c: "ideal (η = 1)"});
    const eta = qeff(R, wl); if (eta > best.eta) best = {eta, wl: wl * 1e9, R}; if (R > peak.R) peak = {R, wl: wl * 1e9};
  }
  return {pts: pts.concat(ideal), best, peak, T, lg};
}

The InGaAs curve rises abruptly at about 920 nm in real devices (slide 33) because the light enters through an InP cap or substrate, which absorbs below 920 nm. Removing the substrate (“visible-extended InGaAs”) restores the short-wavelength response.

TipGoing further
  • Surface recombination. Replace the dead layer \(\ell_p-L_e\) by a proper solution of the diffusion equation in the p⁺ layer with a surface recombination velocity \(S\): solve \(Dn''-n/\tau=-G(x)\) with \(Dn'(0)=Sn(0)\) and \(n=0\) at the depletion edge, derive the collection probability of a carrier generated at depth \(x\), and show that a passivated surface (\(S\to0\)) recovers the blue response. This is what “UV-enhanced” Si photodiodes do.
  • Resonant-cavity photodiodes. Put a thin absorber (\(\alpha d\ll1\)) between two Bragg mirrors (Ch. 1). At resonance the QE becomes \(\eta=\frac{(1+R_2e^{-\alpha d})(1-R_1)(1-e^{-\alpha d})}{(1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}\,e^{-\alpha d})^2}\). A 0.2 µm InGaAs layer can then absorb > 90 %, decoupling QE from transit time (Chapter 5). What is the price?
  • Waveguide photodiodes absorb light travelling along the junction: the absorption length is millimetres while the drift length is 1 µm. They reach 100 GHz with \(\eta>0.8\) and are standard in silicon photonics (Ge-on-Si). Relate the absorption length to the confinement factor Γ of Ch. 2 and 4.
  • Quantum efficiency above 100 %. In Si at λ < 300 nm one photon can create two pairs (impact ionisation by the hot carrier, \(\eta_i>1\)). Why does this not violate energy conservation?

amperes per watt (slides 21–22):

$$

(slide 24). Real photodiodes lose photons by reflection at the surface (transmission $T$), by absorption in regions from which

$$

\qquad \text{pin with thin p}^+:\ I_{ph}\approx\frac{e\eta_iTP_o}{h\nu}\left[1-e^{-\alpha W}\right].

## Going further
- **Surface recombination.** Replace the dead layer $\ell_p-L_e$ by a proper solution of the diffusion equation in the p⁺ layer with
  a surface recombination velocity $S$: solve $Dn''-n/\tau=-G(x)$ with $Dn'(0)=Sn(0)$ and $n=0$ at the depletion edge, derive the
  collection probability of a carrier generated at depth $x$, and show that a passivated surface ($S\to0$) recovers the blue
  response. This is what "UV-enhanced" Si photodiodes do.
- **Resonant-cavity photodiodes.** Put a thin absorber ($\alpha d\ll1$) between two Bragg mirrors (Ch. 1). At resonance the QE becomes
  $\eta=\frac{(1+R_2e^{-\alpha d})(1-R_1)(1-e^{-\alpha d})}{(1-\sqrt{R_1R_2}\,e^{-\alpha d})^2}$. A 0.2 µm InGaAs
  layer can then absorb > 90 %, decoupling QE from transit time (@sec-pin). What is the price?
- **Waveguide photodiodes** absorb light travelling *along* the junction: the absorption length is millimetres while the drift length
  is 1 µm. They reach 100 GHz with $\eta>0.8$ and are standard in silicon photonics (Ge-on-Si). Relate the absorption length to the
  confinement factor Γ of Ch. 2 and 4.
- **Quantum efficiency above 100 %.** In Si at λ < 300 nm one photon can create two pairs (impact ionisation by the hot carrier,
  $\eta_i>1$). Why does this not violate energy conservation?
:::
