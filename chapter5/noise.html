10.1 Key relations

Under constant illumination the photocurrent is not constant: it fluctuates around its mean (slide 86). The noise is quantified by the RMS of the fluctuations, \(i_n=\sqrt{\overline{(i-\bar\imath)^2}}\), within the measurement bandwidth \(B\).

Shot noise. Electrons cross the junction one by one, at random times: the number \(N\) counted in a time \(T\) follows Poisson statistics, \(\sigma_N^2=\bar N\) (slide 87). For a current \(I=e\bar N/T\) measured in a bandwidth \(B=1/2T\), this gives (slides 89–90)

\[ i_{n}^2=2eIB,\qquad i_n=\left[2e(I_d+I_{ph})B\right]^{1/2}. \]

The dark current and the photocurrent both have shot noise. The photocurrent part is called quantum noise: it reflects the random arrival of photons and cannot be removed.

Thermal (Johnson) noise. The carriers in the load resistor have random thermal velocities (slide 88). The available noise power is \(4k_BTB\), so the noise current of \(R_L\) is (slide 92)

\[ i_\text{th}=\left[\frac{4k_BTB}{R_L}\right]^{1/2}. \]

Signal-to-noise ratio. Independent noise sources add in power (mean squares), never in amplitude (slides 93–94):

\[ \text{SNR}=\frac{\text{signal power}}{\text{noise power}}=\frac{I_{ph}^2}{2e(I_d+I_{ph})B+4k_BTB/R_L} . \]

Noise equivalent power and detectivity. The NEP is the optical power that gives SNR = 1 in a 1 Hz bandwidth (slide 95); the detectivity is its inverse, and the specific detectivity normalises by the square root of the area, because for many detectors the noise power scales with area (slide 96):

\[ \text{NEP}=\frac{P_1}{B^{1/2}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\left[2e(I_d+I_{ph})\right]^{1/2}\ [\text{W Hz}^{-1/2}],\qquad D^*=\frac{A^{1/2}}{\text{NEP}}\ [\text{cm Hz}^{1/2}\text{W}^{-1}=\text{Jones}]. \]

For an ideal detector (\(\eta=1\), \(I_d=0\), no thermal noise) the minimum power for SNR = 1 is (slide 101)

\[ P_1=\frac{2hcB}{\eta\lambda}. \]

snOut = {
  const r = rng(sn.seed), counts = d3.range(sn.bins).map(() => r.poisson(sn.N));
  const mean = d3.mean(counts), v = d3.variance(counts);
  const hist = d3.rollups(counts, g => g.length, d => d).map(([k, n]) => ({k, f: n / sn.bins}));
  const fac = (k) => { let s = 0; for (let i = 2; i <= k; i++) s += Math.log(i); return s; };

}

Show the Python 
rxOut = {
  const B = 10 ** rx.lB, RL = 10 ** rx.lRL, Id = 10 ** rx.lId, Pnow = 10 ** rx.lP;
  const parts = (P) => { const Iph = rx.R * P; return {Iph, q: 2 * qe * Iph * B, d: 2 * qe * Id * B, th: 4 * kB * rx.T * B / RL}; };
  const pts = [];
  for (let i = 0; i <= 200; i++) {
    const P = 10 ** (-13 + 10 * i / 200), p = parts(P), tot = p.q + p.d + p.th;
    pts.push({P, v: 10 * Math.log10(p.Iph ** 2 / tot), c: "total SNR"},
             {P, v: 10 * Math.log10(p.Iph ** 2 / p.th), c: "thermal only"},
             {P, v: 10 * Math.log10(p.Iph ** 2 / p.q), c: "quantum only"});
  }
  const p = parts(Pnow), tot = p.q + p.d + p.th;
  const P1 = (() => { let lo = -16, hi = 0; for (let k = 0; k < 80; k++) { const m = (lo + hi) / 2, q = parts(10 ** m); if (q.Iph ** 2 > q.q + q.d + q.th) hi = m; else lo = m; } return 10 ** hi; })();
  return {pts, p, tot, snr: p.Iph ** 2 / tot, P1, B, RL};
}

The default is the receiver of slides 104–105 (InGaAs pin, \(I_d=2\) nA, 1 MHz, 10 kΩ, \(I_{ph}=5\) nA). The thermal noise of the 10 kΩ load is 27 times larger than the shot noise: the receiver is thermal-noise limited, the SNR grows as \(P^2\) (20 dB per decade). At high power the quantum noise takes over and the SNR only grows as \(P\) (10 dB per decade). Raise \(R_L\) to 10 MΩ or cool it: the thermal line moves down. That is why a high transimpedance is the first rule of sensitive receivers, and why it conflicts with bandwidth (Chapter 9).

WarningTwo subtleties hidden in slides 95 and 102
  • NEP of an ideal detector is not a constant. For an ideal detector \(P_1=2hcB/\lambda\) grows linearly with \(B\), so \(\text{NEP}=P_1/\sqrt B\) grows as \(\sqrt B\). The statement “NEP = \(2hc/\lambda\)” of slide 102 is only true numerically at \(B=1\) Hz. The NEP is bandwidth-independent only when the noise does not depend on the signal (dark current, thermal noise): then \(P_1\propto\sqrt B\).
  • The NEP formula contains \(I_{ph}\). In \(\text{NEP}=[2e(I_d+I_{ph})]^{1/2}/\mathcal R\) the photocurrent is that of the power \(P_1\) being sought; the usual datasheet value assumes \(I_{ph}\ll I_d\). It also ignores the amplifier and load, which dominate most real receivers.
TipGoing further
  • Where does \(2eIB\) come from? A train of random δ-pulses of charge \(e\) at rate \(r\) has a flat two-sided power spectral density \(e^2r=eI\) (Campbell’s theorem); the one-sided density is \(2eI\). Verify it numerically: generate Poisson arrivals, compute the periodogram with numpy.fft, and compare with \(2eI\).
  • The noise of a photon stream depends on the source. Laser light far above threshold is Poissonian (coherent state); thermal (chaotic) light within one mode has excess noise \(\sigma_N^2=\bar N+\bar N^2\); “squeezed” light can be quieter than Poisson. Shot noise is therefore a property of the light and of the detection.
  • Background-limited infrared photodetectors (BLIP). In the 3–12 µm range the detector sees the 300 K world (Planck, Ch. 4): the shot noise of the background photocurrent sets \(D^*_\text{BLIP}=\frac{\lambda}{hc}\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{2\Phi_B}}\). Compute \(\Phi_B\) for a detector with a 60° field of view and a 5 µm cut-off, and the resulting \(D^*\). Why do thermal cameras use cold shields?
  • 1/f noise dominates at low frequencies in photoconductors and in dark currents; chopping and lock-in detection
    1. shift the measurement above the 1/f corner.

## Key relations

Under constant illumination the photocurrent is not constant: it fluctuates around its mean (slide 86). The noise is quantified by
the RMS of the fluctuations, $i_n=\sqrt{\overline{(i-\bar\imath)^2}}$, within the measurement bandwidth $B$.

**Shot noise.** Electrons cross the junction one by one, at random times: the number $N$ counted in a time $T$ follows Poisson
statistics, $\sigma_N^2=\bar N$ (slide 87). For a current $I=e\bar N/T$ measured in a bandwidth $B=1/2T$, this gives (slides 89–90)

$$
i_{n}^2=2eIB,\qquad i_n=\left[2e(I_d+I_{ph})B\right]^{1/2}.
$$

The dark current and the photocurrent both have shot noise. The photocurrent part is called **quantum noise**: it reflects the
random arrival of photons and cannot be removed.

**Thermal (Johnson) noise.** The carriers in the load resistor have random thermal velocities (slide 88). The available noise
power is $4k_BTB$, so the noise current of $R_L$ is (slide 92)

$$
i_\text{th}=\left[\frac{4k_BTB}{R_L}\right]^{1/2}.
$$

**Signal-to-noise ratio.** Independent noise sources add in **power** (mean squares), never in amplitude (slides 93–94):

$$
\text{SNR}=\frac{\text{signal power}}{\text{noise power}}=\frac{I_{ph}^2}{2e(I_d+I_{ph})B+4k_BTB/R_L} .
$$

**Noise equivalent power and detectivity.** The NEP is the optical power that gives SNR = 1 in a 1 Hz bandwidth (slide 95); the
detectivity is its inverse, and the **specific detectivity** normalises by the square root of the area, because for many detectors
the noise power scales with area (slide 96):

$$
\text{NEP}=\frac{P_1}{B^{1/2}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal R}\left[2e(I_d+I_{ph})\right]^{1/2}\ [\text{W Hz}^{-1/2}],\qquad
D^*=\frac{A^{1/2}}{\text{NEP}}\ [\text{cm Hz}^{1/2}\text{W}^{-1}=\text{Jones}].
$$

For an **ideal** detector ($\eta=1$, $I_d=0$, no thermal noise) the minimum power for SNR = 1 is (slide 101)

$$
P_1=\frac{2hcB}{\eta\lambda}.
$$

The default is the receiver of slides 104–105 (InGaAs pin, $I_d=2$ nA, 1 MHz, 10 kΩ, $I_{ph}=5$ nA). The thermal noise of the
10 kΩ load is 27 times larger than the shot noise: the receiver is **thermal-noise limited**, the SNR grows as $P^2$ (20 dB per
decade). At high power the quantum noise takes over and the SNR only grows as $P$ (10 dB per decade). Raise $R_L$ to 10 MΩ or cool it:
the thermal line moves down. That is why a high transimpedance is the first rule of sensitive receivers, and why it conflicts with
bandwidth (@sec-circuits).

::: {.callout-warning collapse="true"}
## Two subtleties hidden in slides 95 and 102
- **NEP of an ideal detector is not a constant.** For an ideal detector $P_1=2hcB/\lambda$ grows *linearly* with $B$, so
  $\text{NEP}=P_1/\sqrt B$ grows as $\sqrt B$. The statement "NEP = $2hc/\lambda$" of slide 102 is only true numerically at
  $B=1$ Hz. The NEP is bandwidth-independent only when the noise does not depend on the signal (dark current, thermal noise): then
  $P_1\propto\sqrt B$.
- **The NEP formula contains $I_{ph}$.** In $\text{NEP}=[2e(I_d+I_{ph})]^{1/2}/\mathcal R$ the photocurrent is that of the power
  $P_1$ being sought; the usual datasheet value assumes $I_{ph}\ll I_d$. It also ignores the amplifier and load, which dominate most
  real receivers.
:::

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Going further
- **Where does $2eIB$ come from?** A train of random δ-pulses of charge $e$ at rate $r$ has a flat two-sided power spectral density
  $e^2r=eI$ (Campbell's theorem); the one-sided density is $2eI$. Verify it numerically: generate Poisson arrivals, compute the
  periodogram with `numpy.fft`, and compare with $2eI$.
- **The noise of a photon stream depends on the source.** Laser light far above threshold is Poissonian (coherent state); thermal
  (chaotic) light within one mode has excess noise $\sigma_N^2=\bar N+\bar N^2$; "squeezed" light can be quieter than Poisson. Shot
  noise is therefore a property of the light *and* of the detection.
- **Background-limited infrared photodetectors (BLIP).** In the 3–12 µm range the detector sees the 300 K world (Planck, Ch. 4): the
  shot noise of the background photocurrent sets $D^*_\text{BLIP}=\frac{\lambda}{hc}\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{2\Phi_B}}$. Compute $\Phi_B$ for a
  detector with a 60° field of view and a 5 µm cut-off, and the resulting $D^*$. Why do thermal cameras use cold shields?
- **1/f noise** dominates at low frequencies in photoconductors and in dark currents; chopping and lock-in detection
  (@sec-photoconductors) shift the measurement above the 1/f corner.
:::
