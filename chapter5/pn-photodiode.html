1.1 Key relations

A photon with \(h\nu>E_g\) absorbed in a semiconductor creates an electron–hole pair (EHP). If it is created in the depletion region (space-charge layer, SCL) of a pn junction, the built-in field \(E_o\) separates the pair at once: the electron rolls down \(E_c\) to the n side and the hole rolls up \(E_v\) to the p side (slide 6). If it is created in a neutral region, the minority carrier must first diffuse to the SCL; it is collected only if it gets there before it recombines, i.e. within about one diffusion length \(L_e\) or \(L_h\) (slide 7, Ch. 3).

Each collected pair moves one electron charge around the external circuit. The photodiode is therefore a current source \(I_{ph}\) in parallel with the ordinary diode of Ch. 3. With the sign convention of slide 10 (positive \(I\) and \(V\) as in forward bias),

\[ I=I_o\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{eV}{\eta k_BT}\right)-1\right]-I_{ph},\qquad I_{ph}=\mathcal{R}P_o , \]

where \(\mathcal R\) is the responsivity (Chapter 4). The illuminated curve is the dark curve shifted down by \(I_{ph}\). Three operating points matter:

  • Reverse bias (\(V=-V_r\), third quadrant): \(I\approx-I_{ph}-I_o\), a current proportional to the power. This is the photodiode (photoconductive) mode used for detection: linear, fast (wide SCL, small capacitance), but with a dark current.
  • Short circuit (\(V=0\)): \(I_{sc}=-I_{ph}\) (slide 8). Still linear, and no bias means almost no dark current.
  • Open circuit (\(I=0\), fourth quadrant): the separated carriers charge the n side negative and the p side positive. This forward voltage lowers the barrier until diffusion back across the junction cancels the photocurrent (slide 9):

\[ V_{oc}=\frac{\eta k_BT}{e}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{I_{ph}}{I_o}\right). \]

With a load the photodiode delivers power: this is the photovoltaic mode of the solar cell (Chapter 13).

  eta: Inputs.range([1, 2], {value: 1, step: 0.05, label: "ideality factor η"}),

Show the Python 
pnOut = {
  const Io = 10 ** pn.lIo, Iph = pn.R * pn.P * 1e-6, Vt = pn.eta * kT(300), dark = [], light = [];
  const Vmax = Math.max(0.15, Vt * Math.log(1 + 2 * Math.max(Iph, 1e-6) / Io));
  for (let i = 0; i <= 600; i++) {
    const V = -Math.max(pn.Vr, 1) - 0.5 + (Vmax + Math.max(pn.Vr, 1) + 0.5) * i / 600;
    dark.push({V, I: 1e6 * Io * Math.expm1(V / Vt), c: "dark"});
    light.push({V, I: 1e6 * (Io * Math.expm1(V / Vt) - Iph), c: "illuminated"});
  }
  const Voc = Vt * Math.log1p(Iph / Io);
  return {pts: dark.concat(light), Iph, Io, Voc, Ir: Io * Math.expm1(-pn.Vr / Vt) - Iph, Vmax};
}
Plot.plot({
  width: Math.min(width, 1050), height: 320, x: {label: "diode voltage V (V)"}, y: {label: "current I (µA)", grid: true,
    domain: [-1.3 * Math.max(pnOut.Iph * 1e6, 1), 0.8 * Math.max(pnOut.Iph * 1e6, 1)]},
  color: {legend: true, domain: ["dark", "illuminated"], range: ["#52514e", "#e34948"]},
  marks: [Plot.ruleX([0]), Plot.ruleY([0]),
          Plot.line(pnOut.pts, {x: "V", y: "I", stroke: "c", strokeWidth: 2, clip: true}),

          Plot.dot([{V: pnOut.Voc, I: 0}], {x: "V", y: "I", r: 6, fill: "#1baf7a", title: () => "open circuit"}),
          Plot.dot([{V: 0, I: -1e6 * pnOut.Iph}], {x: "V", y: "I", r: 6, fill: "#eda100", title: () => "short circuit"})]
})

Move the power slider: the reverse current and \(I_{sc}\) follow \(P_o\) linearly, while \(V_{oc}\) grows only by \(\eta k_BT\ln 10\) (60 mV for \(\eta=1\)) per decade of power. Raise \(I_o\) (a larger or hotter diode, a smaller band gap) and watch \(V_{oc}\) fall. This is why germanium and InGaAs photodiodes, with dark currents in the µA range (slide 49), make poor photovoltaic detectors and why InAs and InSb must be cooled.

1.3 Worked example: photodiode or photovoltaic mode?

A Si photodiode of 1 mm² has \(I_o=0.1\) nA and \(\mathcal R=0.5\) A/W at 850 nm. Compare its output for 1 nW, 1 µW and 1 mW in reverse bias and in open circuit.

Show the Python 
rows = []
for P in (1e-9, 1e-6, 1e-3):
    Iph = 0.5*P
    rows.append((f"{P:.0e} W", f"{Iph:.1e} A", f"{dl.kT()*np.log1p(Iph/1e-10)*1e3:.1f} mV"))
pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["P_o", "I (reverse bias) ≈ I_ph", "V_oc"])

The reverse-biased photodiode is a linear power meter; the open-circuit photodiode is a logarithmic one. The first is the basis of every optical receiver; the second is useful for wide-dynamic-range light meters and is the operating principle of the solar cell.

TipGoing further
  • Where is the dark current coming from? In reverse bias \(I_o\) has a diffusion part \(\propto n_i^2\) and a generation part in the SCL \(\propto n_iW/\tau_g\) (Ch. 3). Which dominates in Si at room temperature, and how does each scale with temperature and band gap? Estimate the increase of the dark current of a Si photodiode between 25 °C and 75 °C.
  • Zero-bias (photovoltaic-mode) detection is used in precision photometry: there is no dark current, only the thermal (Johnson) noise of the shunt resistance \(R_p=\eta k_BT/eI_o\). Compute \(R_p\) for \(I_o=10\) pA and compare its noise with the shot noise of 10 pA in reverse bias (Chapter 10).
  • Photon recycling in the neutral regions. The collection probability of a carrier generated at a distance \(x\) from the SCL is \(\approx e^{-x/L}\). Combine it with \(e^{-\alpha x}\) to obtain the internal QE of a pn photodiode (Chapter 4).

## Key relations

A photon with $h\nu>E_g$ absorbed in a semiconductor creates an electron–hole pair (EHP). If it is created in the **depletion
region** (space-charge layer, SCL) of a pn junction, the built-in field $E_o$ separates the pair at once: the electron rolls down
$E_c$ to the n side and the hole rolls up $E_v$ to the p side (slide 6). If it is created in a **neutral region**, the minority
carrier must first diffuse to the SCL; it is collected only if it gets there before it recombines, i.e. within about one
diffusion length $L_e$ or $L_h$ (slide 7, Ch. 3).

Each collected pair moves one electron charge around the external circuit. The photodiode is therefore a current source
$I_{ph}$ in parallel with the ordinary diode of Ch. 3. With the sign convention of slide 10 (positive $I$ and $V$ as in forward
bias),

$$
I=I_o\left[\exp\!\left(\frac{eV}{\eta k_BT}\right)-1\right]-I_{ph},\qquad I_{ph}=\mathcal{R}P_o ,
$$

where $\mathcal R$ is the responsivity (@sec-responsivity). The illuminated curve is the dark curve shifted down by $I_{ph}$.
Three operating points matter:

- **Reverse bias** ($V=-V_r$, third quadrant): $I\approx-I_{ph}-I_o$, a current proportional to the power. This is the
  **photodiode** (photoconductive) mode used for detection: linear, fast (wide SCL, small capacitance), but with a dark current.
- **Short circuit** ($V=0$): $I_{sc}=-I_{ph}$ (slide 8). Still linear, and no bias means almost no dark current.
- **Open circuit** ($I=0$, fourth quadrant): the separated carriers charge the n side negative and the p side positive. This
  forward voltage lowers the barrier until diffusion back across the junction cancels the photocurrent (slide 9):

$$
V_{oc}=\frac{\eta k_BT}{e}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{I_{ph}}{I_o}\right).
$$

With a load the photodiode delivers power: this is the **photovoltaic** mode of the solar cell (@sec-solar).
