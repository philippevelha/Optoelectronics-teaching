11.1 Key relations

An APD multiplies the primary photocurrent and dark current by \(M\). If every primary carrier produced exactly \(M\) secondaries, the shot noise would be multiplied too, \(i_n=M[2e(I_{do}+I_{pho})B]^{1/2}\), and the SNR would be unchanged (slide 99). But impact ionisation is random: some carriers travel far before ionising, others ionise at once (slide 100). The gain itself fluctuates, and the noise is larger by the excess noise factor \(F=\langle M^2\rangle/\langle M\rangle^2\):

\[ i_{n\text{-APD}}=\left[2e(I_{do}+I_{pho})M^2FB\right]^{1/2},\qquad F\approx M^x . \]

\(x\approx0.3\)–\(0.5\) for Si, \(0.7\)–\(1\) for Ge and InGaAs/InP (slide 100). McIntyre’s theory for a uniform avalanche region, with the avalanche started by the carrier of larger coefficient and \(k\) the ratio of the smaller to the larger coefficient, gives

\[ F=kM+(1-k)\left(2-\frac1M\right), \]

so \(F\to2\) at high gain when \(k=0\), and \(F\approx M\) when \(k=1\). The SNR of an APD receiver with a load \(R_L\) is

\[ \text{SNR}=\frac{M^2I_{pho}^2}{2e(I_{do}+I_{pho})M^2FB+4k_BTB/R_L}. \]

Gain helps against the thermal noise of the load and amplifier, which is not multiplied, but it adds excess noise to the shot noise. There is therefore an optimum gain: above it, \(M^2F\) grows faster than \(M^2\) and the SNR falls.

TipGoing further
  • Optimum gain analytically. With \(F=M^x\) and dark current neglected, maximise the SNR: \(M_\text{opt}^{2+x}=\frac{4k_BT/R_L}{xe(I_{pho}+I_{do})}\). Check it against the panel. Why does the optimum decrease when the signal increases?
  • Gain–bandwidth product. With \(k>0\) the avalanche takes several round trips of electrons and holes to build up; at high gain the bandwidth falls as \(f_{3dB}\approx1/(2\pi M k\tau_{av})\), a constant gain–bandwidth product (100–300 GHz for InGaAs/InAlAs APDs). Small \(k\) is good for noise and speed.
  • Electron-APDs. HgCdTe and InAs APDs have \(k\approx0\) and \(F\approx1\)–\(1.3\) up to \(M=100\): the avalanche is deterministic, almost a noiseless amplifier. They are used for single-photon-sensitive infrared imaging.
  • Compare with an optical preamplifier (Ch. 4): an EDFA before a pin has a noise figure of about 3 dB, i.e. an effective “\(F\)” of 2 with \(M=G\approx1000\) and THz of optical bandwidth (Chapter 14).

  bandwidth falls as $f_{3dB}\approx1/(2\pi M k\tau_{av})$, a constant gain–bandwidth product (100–300 GHz for InGaAs/InAlAs APDs).
  Small $k$ is good for noise *and* speed.
- **Electron-APDs.** HgCdTe and InAs APDs have $k\approx0$ and $F\approx1$–$1.3$ up to $M=100$: the avalanche is deterministic, almost a
  noiseless amplifier. They are used for single-photon-sensitive infrared imaging.
- **Compare with an optical preamplifier** (Ch. 4): an EDFA before a pin has a noise figure of about 3 dB, i.e. an effective "$F$" of 2
  with $M=G\approx1000$ and THz of optical bandwidth (@sec-receivers).
:::

## Try it yourself

1. Derive McIntyre's limits: $F(M\to\infty)=2$ for $k=0$, $F=M$ for $k=1$. Interpret $F=2$ physically.
2. What value of $x$ in $F=M^x$ reproduces McIntyre with $k=0.5$ at $M=10$?
3. In the panel, set the load to 50 Ω and the bandwidth to 10 GHz. Is an APD with $x=0.7$ worth it? How much does an APD with
   $k=0.1$ gain instead?
4. A colleague doubles the APD gain from 10 to 20 "to double the signal". When does this improve and when does it degrade the SNR?
