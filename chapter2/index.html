Preface

“The introduction of optical fiber systems will revolutionize the communications network. The low-transmission loss and the large bandwidth capability of the fiber systems allow signals to be transmitted for establishing communications contacts over large distances with few or no provisions of intermediate amplification.” — Charles K. Kao, 1982 (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2009)

This companion follows the slides of Chapter 2 — Dielectric Waveguides and Optical Fibers section by section. The slides tell the story; this book lets you compute it. Every figure is produced by a few lines of Python that you can unfold (“Show the Python”), copy, and modify. Most chapters also have live panels (sliders, marked with a blue bar on the left) that recompute directly in your browser. They need no Python installation.

A bit of history (slides 1–5)

Show the Python 
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import fiberlib as fl
fl.use_style()

events = [(1842, "Colladon:\nwater jet"), (1854, "Tyndall\nlecture"),
          (1955, "Kapany:\nPhD, 'fiber optics'"), (1966, "Kao & Hockam:\n< 20 dB/km predicted"),
          (1970, "Corning:\nfirst low-loss fiber"), (1987, "EDFA"),
          (1996, "Russell:\nphotonic crystal fiber"), (2009, "Nobel Prize\n(Kao)")]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 2.4))
ax.axhline(0, color=fl.INK2, lw=1)
for i, (y, txt) in enumerate(events):
    up = 1 if i % 2 == 0 else -1
    ax.plot([y, y], [0, 0.55 * up], color=fl.PALETTE[0], lw=1)
    ax.plot(y, 0, "o", ms=6, color=fl.PALETTE[0])
    ax.text(y, 0.62 * up, f"{y}\n{txt}" if up > 0 else f"{txt}\n{y}",
            ha="center", va="bottom" if up > 0 else "top", fontsize=8.5, color=fl.INK)
ax.set_ylim(-1.6, 1.6); ax.set_xlim(1830, 2020)
ax.axis("off")
plt.show()

The time axis is not linear in “interest”: it took more than a century to go from Colladon’s water jet to a usable glass fiber. The breakthrough was materials purity (Kao’s prediction that intrinsic glass losses could fall below 20 dB/km), not a new idea about guiding. The chapter Attenuation & bending shows why: today’s 0.15–0.2 dB/km sit close to the Rayleigh limit of silica.
