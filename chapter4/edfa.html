4.1 Key relations

An ion in the lower level absorbs a fraction of the light that crosses it, as if it presented an opaque area, the absorption cross section \(\sigma_{ab}\) (slide 22). An ion in the upper level adds stimulated photons with an emission cross section \(\sigma_{em}\) (slide 23). In a slab of thickness \(\delta x\), with \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) ions per unit volume, the intensity changes by \(\delta I/I=(\sigma_{em}N_2-\sigma_{ab}N_1)\delta x\). This defines the optical gain coefficient (slide 24),

\[ g(\nu)=\sigma_{em}(\nu)N_2-\sigma_{ab}(\nu)N_1,\qquad G=\frac{P_\text{out}}{P_\text{in}}=\exp(gL). \]

In the EDFA (slides 25–28), Er³⁺ ions in silica glass absorb a 980 nm pump into the \(^4I_{11/2}\) level, which decays in microseconds to the metastable \(^4I_{13/2}\) level (\(\tau_{sp}\approx10\) ms). Signals in the C band (1530–1565 nm) are amplified by stimulated emission back to the ground state \(^4I_{15/2}\). Each level is split into a manifold of Stark sublevels, so \(\sigma_{ab}(\lambda)\) and \(\sigma_{em}(\lambda)\) are broad and different: absorption dominates at short wavelengths, emission at long ones (slide 28a). With the fractional inversion \(x=N_2/N_0\) (slide 39),

\[ g=\left[\sigma_{em}x-\sigma_{ab}(1-x)\right]N_0 . \]

Energy conservation bounds the output (slides 36–37). The power conversion efficiency \(\eta_\text{PCE}=(P_{s,\text{out}}-P_{s,\text{in}})/P_{p,\text{in}}\) cannot exceed \(\lambda_p/\lambda_s\) (one signal photon per pump photon at best), so

\[ G=1+\eta_\text{PCE}\frac{P_{p,\text{in}}}{P_{s,\text{in}}},\qquad P_{s,\text{in}}<\frac{\lambda_p}{\lambda_s}\,\frac{P_{p,\text{in}}}{G-1}. \]

The pump is absorbed as it travels. It can keep all the ions in a length \(L_p\) inverted only if it delivers \(N_0h\nu_p\) per unit volume every \(\tau_{sp}\) (slide 38): \(\Gamma P_p\approx AN_0h\nu_pL_p/\tau_{sp}\), with \(\Gamma\) the fraction of pump power that overlaps the doped core.

The slide’s warning holds: losing 10 % of the inversion costs 7 dB of gain, because the 10 % of ions left in the ground state absorb. The small-signal gain at full inversion (41.7 dB) is an upper bound that is never reached.

4.3 The EDFA along the fiber

The uniform-inversion model ignores that the pump weakens along the fiber. The panel solves the steady-state two-level rate equations at every point. The local inversion is set by the local pump and signal photon fluxes,

\[ x(z)=\frac{\sigma_{ab,p}\Phi_p+\sigma_{ab,s}\Phi_s}{(\sigma_{ab,p}+\sigma_{em,p})\Phi_p+(\sigma_{ab,s}+\sigma_{em,s})\Phi_s+1/\tau_{sp}}, \qquad \Phi_k=\frac{\Gamma_kP_k}{h\nu_kA}, \]

and each power grows or decays as \(dP_k/dz=\Gamma_k[\sigma_{em,k}x-\sigma_{ab,k}(1-x)]N_0P_k\) (forward pumping, no ASE).

4.4 Gain flattening (slides 41–42)

In a WDM system (Ch. 2) every channel crosses tens of amplifiers. A 1 dB gain difference per amplifier becomes 30 dB after 30 spans. Gain-flattened EDFAs combine a well-chosen average inversion with a passive filter (long-period or chirped fiber Bragg gratings, slides 41–42) whose loss is the mirror image of the gain:

band = (wl > 1530e-9) & (wl < 1562e-9)
filt = np.where(band, GdB - GdB[band].min(), np.nan)
ax[0].axvspan(1530, 1562, color=lz.GRID, alpha=0.5, zorder=0)

flattened gain 9.9 dB; filter must remove up to 3.5 dB

Flattening throws away the excess gain near 1530 nm. The price is pump power, and the flat shape holds only at one inversion, that is, at one total input power. Amplifiers in reconfigurable networks therefore control their gain electronically (automatic gain control) or add a variable attenuator.

4.5 Going further: noise figure and amplified spontaneous emission (slides 43–44)

Slide 43 defines the noise figure \(\text{NF}=\text{SNR}_\text{in}/\text{SNR}_\text{out}\) but does not say where the noise comes from. Ions in the upper level also emit spontaneously into the guided mode. These photons are amplified like the signal, and form the broad amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) pedestal of slide 44a. In a bandwidth \(B_o\) and two polarisations, the ASE power at the output is

\[ P_\text{ASE}=2n_{sp}(G-1)h\nu B_o,\qquad n_{sp}=\frac{\sigma_{em}N_2}{\sigma_{em}N_2-\sigma_{ab}N_1}\ge1 . \]

The spontaneous-emission factor \(n_{sp}\) equals 1 only at full inversion. When the amplified signal beats with the ASE on a photodiode (Ch. 5), the dominant noise is the signal–spontaneous beat noise, and

\[ \text{NF}\approx2n_{sp}\frac{G-1}{G}+\frac1G\ \xrightarrow{G\gg1}\ 2n_{sp}\ \ge\ 2\ (3\text{ dB}). \]

No phase-insensitive amplifier can do better than 3 dB: amplifying a coherent state necessarily adds noise (Caves, 1982).

The noise figure is lowest where the inversion is highest, which is at the pump input end of the fiber. This is why low-noise pre-amplifiers are forward pumped at 980 nm (full inversion possible) while booster amplifiers can be backward pumped or use 1480 nm pumps (higher PCE, but \(x<1\) because the pump also stimulates emission). Slide 44b shows the other effect: when the input power rises, the signal saturates the inversion, \(n_{sp}\) grows and the NF increases.

In a chain of amplifiers the noise figures add like those of electronic amplifiers (Friis): \(\text{NF}_\text{tot}=\text{NF}_1+(\text{NF}_2-1)/G_1+\dots\) The first amplifier dominates, so it must have high gain and low NF.

TipGoing further
  • Include the ASE in the model. Add forward and backward ASE powers in bins of 1 nm to edfa_propagate (Giles–Desurvire model). The ASE depletes the inversion at high gain and limits the useful gain to about 40 dB. Solve the two-point boundary problem with scipy.integrate.solve_bvp.
  • Pumping configurations (slides 29–32). edfa_propagate(..., backward_pump=True) solves counter-propagating pumping. Compare the gain and the inversion at the input end (which sets the NF) for forward and backward pumping with the same pump power.
  • Gain dynamics. The 10 ms lifetime makes the EDFA gain too slow to follow individual bits at Gb/s: no pattern effects, unlike the SOA (Chapter 15). But adding or dropping WDM channels causes gain transients in the surviving channels within 10–100 µs.
  • Other amplifiers. Raman amplification uses the silica fiber itself (gain peak 13 THz below the pump, any band). Thulium (S band, 2 µm), praseodymium (O band) and bismuth-doped fibers cover other windows.

## Key relations

An ion in the lower level absorbs a fraction of the light that crosses it, as if it presented an opaque area, the **absorption
cross section** $\sigma_{ab}$ (slide 22). An ion in the upper level adds stimulated photons with an **emission cross section**
$\sigma_{em}$ (slide 23). In a slab of thickness $\delta x$, with $N_1$ and $N_2$ ions per unit volume, the intensity changes by
$\delta I/I=(\sigma_{em}N_2-\sigma_{ab}N_1)\delta x$. This defines the **optical gain coefficient** (slide 24),

$$
g(\nu)=\sigma_{em}(\nu)N_2-\sigma_{ab}(\nu)N_1,\qquad G=\frac{P_\text{out}}{P_\text{in}}=\exp(gL).
$$

In the EDFA (slides 25–28), Er³⁺ ions in silica glass absorb a 980 nm pump into the $^4I_{11/2}$ level, which decays in
microseconds to the metastable $^4I_{13/2}$ level ($\tau_{sp}\approx10$ ms). Signals in the C band (1530–1565 nm) are amplified by
stimulated emission back to the ground state $^4I_{15/2}$. Each level is split into a manifold of Stark sublevels, so
$\sigma_{ab}(\lambda)$ and $\sigma_{em}(\lambda)$ are broad and different: absorption dominates at short wavelengths, emission at
long ones (slide 28a). With the fractional inversion $x=N_2/N_0$ (slide 39),

$$
g=\left[\sigma_{em}x-\sigma_{ab}(1-x)\right]N_0 .
$$

Energy conservation bounds the output (slides 36–37). The **power conversion efficiency**
$\eta_\text{PCE}=(P_{s,\text{out}}-P_{s,\text{in}})/P_{p,\text{in}}$ cannot exceed $\lambda_p/\lambda_s$ (one signal photon per pump photon
at best), so

$$
G=1+\eta_\text{PCE}\frac{P_{p,\text{in}}}{P_{s,\text{in}}},\qquad
P_{s,\text{in}}<\frac{\lambda_p}{\lambda_s}\,\frac{P_{p,\text{in}}}{G-1}.
$$

The pump is absorbed as it travels. It can keep all the ions in a length $L_p$ inverted only if it delivers $N_0h\nu_p$ per unit
volume every $\tau_{sp}$ (slide 38): $\Gamma P_p\approx AN_0h\nu_pL_p/\tau_{sp}$, with $\Gamma$ the fraction of pump power that overlaps
the doped core.

## The EDFA along the fiber

The uniform-inversion model ignores that the pump weakens along the fiber. The panel solves the steady-state two-level rate
equations at every point. The local inversion is set by the local pump and signal photon fluxes,

$$
x(z)=\frac{\sigma_{ab,p}\Phi_p+\sigma_{ab,s}\Phi_s}{(\sigma_{ab,p}+\sigma_{em,p})\Phi_p+(\sigma_{ab,s}+\sigma_{em,s})\Phi_s+1/\tau_{sp}},
\qquad \Phi_k=\frac{\Gamma_kP_k}{h\nu_kA},
$$

and each power grows or decays as $dP_k/dz=\Gamma_k[\sigma_{em,k}x-\sigma_{ab,k}(1-x)]N_0P_k$ (forward pumping, no ASE).

## Gain flattening (slides 41–42)

In a WDM system (Ch. 2) every channel crosses tens of amplifiers. A 1 dB gain difference per amplifier becomes 30 dB after 30
spans. Gain-flattened EDFAs combine a well-chosen average inversion with a passive filter (long-period or chirped fiber Bragg
gratings, slides 41–42) whose loss is the mirror image of the gain:

```{python}
#| label: fig-flatten
#| fig-cap: "Gain spectrum of the 3 m EDFA of slide 39 (N₀ = 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, Γ = 0.7) for three average inversions, and the loss filter that flattens the x = 0.65 curve to its minimum over 1530–1562 nm (a 'long-period grating' in the language of slide 42)."
wl = np.linspace(1520e-9, 1570e-9, 400)
ab, em = lz.er_cross_sections(wl)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3.6))
for x in (0.55, 0.65, 0.75):
    g = 0.7*(em*x - ab*(1 - x))*1e25
    ax[0].plot(wl*1e9, 4.343*g*3, label=f"x = {x}")
x = 0.65; GdB = 4.343*0.7*(em*x - ab*(1 - x))*1e25*3
band = (wl > 1530e-9) & (wl < 1562e-9)
filt = np.where(band, GdB - GdB[band].min(), np.nan)
ax[0].axvspan(1530, 1562, color=lz.GRID, alpha=0.5, zorder=0)
ax[0].set(xlabel="wavelength (nm)", ylabel="gain (dB)"); ax[0].legend(fontsize=8)
ax[1].plot(wl*1e9, -filt, color=lz.PALETTE[7], label="filter transmission (dB)")
ax[1].plot(wl*1e9, np.where(band, GdB - filt, np.nan), color=lz.PALETTE[2], label="flattened gain (dB)")
ax[1].set(xlabel="wavelength (nm)", ylabel="dB"); ax[1].legend(fontsize=8); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
print(f"flattened gain {GdB[band].min():.1f} dB; filter must remove up to {np.nanmax(filt):.1f} dB")
```

Flattening throws away the excess gain near 1530 nm. The price is pump power, and the flat shape holds only at one inversion,
that is, at one total input power. Amplifiers in reconfigurable networks therefore control their gain electronically (automatic
gain control) or add a variable attenuator.

## Going further: noise figure and amplified spontaneous emission (slides 43–44) {#sec-edfa-noise}

Slide 43 defines the noise figure $\text{NF}=\text{SNR}_\text{in}/\text{SNR}_\text{out}$ but does not say where the noise comes
from. Ions in the upper level also emit **spontaneously** into the guided mode. These photons are amplified like the signal, and
form the broad **amplified spontaneous emission** (ASE) pedestal of slide 44a. In a bandwidth $B_o$ and two polarisations, the
ASE power at the output is

$$
P_\text{ASE}=2n_{sp}(G-1)h\nu B_o,\qquad n_{sp}=\frac{\sigma_{em}N_2}{\sigma_{em}N_2-\sigma_{ab}N_1}\ge1 .
$$

The **spontaneous-emission factor** $n_{sp}$ equals 1 only at full inversion. When the amplified signal beats with the ASE on a
photodiode (Ch. 5), the dominant noise is the **signal–spontaneous beat noise**, and

$$
\text{NF}\approx2n_{sp}\frac{G-1}{G}+\frac1G\ \xrightarrow{G\gg1}\ 2n_{sp}\ \ge\ 2\ (3\text{ dB}).
$$

No phase-insensitive amplifier can do better than 3 dB: amplifying a coherent state necessarily adds noise (Caves, 1982).

The noise figure is lowest where the inversion is highest, which is at the **pump input end** of the fiber. This is why
low-noise pre-amplifiers are forward pumped at 980 nm (full inversion possible) while booster amplifiers can be backward pumped or
use 1480 nm pumps (higher PCE, but $x<1$ because the pump also stimulates emission). Slide 44b shows the other effect: when the
input power rises, the signal saturates the inversion, $n_{sp}$ grows and the NF increases.

In a chain of amplifiers the noise figures add like those of electronic amplifiers (Friis):
$\text{NF}_\text{tot}=\text{NF}_1+(\text{NF}_2-1)/G_1+\dots$ The first amplifier dominates, so it must have high gain and low NF.

## Going further
- **Include the ASE in the model.** Add forward and backward ASE powers in bins of 1 nm to `edfa_propagate` (Giles–Desurvire
  model). The ASE depletes the inversion at high gain and limits the useful gain to about 40 dB. Solve the two-point boundary
  problem with `scipy.integrate.solve_bvp`.
- **Pumping configurations** (slides 29–32). `edfa_propagate(..., backward_pump=True)` solves counter-propagating pumping. Compare
  the gain and the inversion at the input end (which sets the NF) for forward and backward pumping with the same pump power.
- **Gain dynamics.** The 10 ms lifetime makes the EDFA gain too slow to follow individual bits at Gb/s: no pattern effects, unlike
  the SOA (@sec-soa). But adding or dropping WDM channels causes gain **transients** in the surviving channels within 10–100 µs.
- **Other amplifiers.** Raman amplification uses the silica fiber itself (gain peak 13 THz below the pump, any band). Thulium
  (S band, 2 µm), praseodymium (O band) and bismuth-doped fibers cover other windows.
