9.1 Key relations

In a semiconductor the “two levels” are two bands. Under forward bias, electrons injected into the conduction band and holes in the valence band each reach their own equilibrium, described by the quasi-Fermi levels \(E_{Fn}\) and \(E_{Fp}\) of Chapter 3, with \(E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}=eV\) (slide 90). A photon of energy \(h\nu\) connects a conduction state \(E_2\) with a valence state \(E_1\) with \(E_2-E_1=h\nu\). Stimulated emission beats absorption when the upper state is more likely full than the lower state, \(f_c(E_2)>f_v(E_1)\), which reduces to the Bernard–Duraffourg condition

\[ E_g<h\nu<E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}=eV . \]

The quasi-Fermi levels must penetrate into the bands: in a homojunction this requires degenerate doping on both sides (slide 90); in a double heterostructure the injected carriers themselves do it (Chapter 10). The gain spectrum (slide 91) is the absorption spectrum of the unpumped crystal multiplied by the inversion factor,

\[ g(h\nu)=\alpha_0(h\nu)\left[f_c(E_2)-f_v(E_1)\right], \]

with \(\alpha_0\propto\sqrt{h\nu-E_g}\) for parabolic bands. Gain is positive between \(E_g\) and \(\Delta F=E_{Fn}-E_{Fp}\), zero at \(h\nu=\Delta F\) (transparency of that photon energy) and negative (absorption) above. As the carrier density \(n\) grows, \(\Delta F\) moves up, the gain band widens and its peak shifts to higher energies (slide 129).

9.2 Gain spectrum from the quasi-Fermi levels

The panel computes \(E_{Fn}\) and \(E_{Fp}\) from the injected density \(n=p\) (undoped active layer, Fermi–Dirac statistics) and plots the free-carrier gain. It uses parabolic bands with only heavy holes and no broadening: it gets the shape and the order of magnitude of the transparency density, but overestimates the peak gain by a factor of 3 to 10 (see below).

gpOut = {
  const S = semis[gp.mat], n = gp.n*1e24, Fn = quasiFermi(n, S.mE, gp.T), Fp = quasiFermi(n, S.mH, gp.T);
  const dF = S.Eg + Fn + Fp, par = {Eg: S.Eg, mE: S.mE, mH: S.mH, Fn, Fp, T: gp.T}, data = [];

  const pk = data.reduce((m, d) => d.g > m.g ? d : m, data[0]);
  return {data, Fn, Fp, dF, pk, Eg: S.Eg};
}

The free-carrier model shows the physics but overestimates the gain: it neglects the light-hole band, intraband scattering (which broadens each transition by about 10 meV), many-body effects such as bandgap renormalisation, and the polarisation dependence of the matrix element. For design numbers, the slides use measured or fully modelled curves (slide 130), and so does the example of Chapter 12. Near threshold, a linear or logarithmic fit is common:

\[ g_\text{peak}\approx a\,(n-n_{tr})\quad\text{or}\quad g_\text{peak}\approx g_0\ln\frac{n}{n_{tr}}, \]

with the differential gain \(a=dg/dn\approx1\)–\(3\times10^{-16}\) cm² for bulk GaAs and transparency density \(n_{tr}\approx1\)–\(1.5\times10^{18}\) cm⁻³. The differential gain sets the modulation speed (Chapter 16).

TipGoing further
  • Add the light holes. The valence band has heavy (\(m^*\approx0.5m_e\)) and light (\(m^*\approx0.08m_e\)) holes. Compute \(E_{Fp}\) with both bands and add the two gain contributions. How much does the transparency density change?
  • Gain and spontaneous emission are linked. The same Fermi factors give the spontaneous spectrum \(r_{sp}\propto\alpha_0f_c(1-f_v)\), and \(g(h\nu)=r_{sp}(h\nu)\,[1-e^{(h\nu-\Delta F)/k_BT}]\times\text{const}\) (McCumber/van Roosbroeck–Shockley). Measuring the spontaneous spectrum gives the gain (Hakki–Paoli method uses the FP fringes instead).
  • Refractive index change. Gain and refractive index are linked by Kramers–Kronig. The change \(dn/dN\) of the index with carrier density causes the linewidth enhancement factor \(\alpha_H\) and the chirp of directly modulated lasers (Chapter 19).
