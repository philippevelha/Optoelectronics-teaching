10.1 Key relations

The first laser diodes (Hall et al., 1962, slide 93) were GaAs homojunctions: a degenerately doped pn junction with two cleaved facets as mirrors (slide 92). The facets reflect \(R=[(n-1)/(n+1)]^2\approx0.32\), enough because the gain is huge. But the injected carriers spread over a diffusion length (µm) and the light is not guided, so the threshold current density was about 500 A mm⁻² at room temperature (slide 94 notes): only pulsed operation, or cryogenic cooling.

The double heterostructure (DH, slides 95–96) sandwiches a thin (0.1–0.2 µm) narrow-gap active layer between wide-gap claddings. The band offsets confine the carriers, and the higher index of the active layer guides the light (the planar waveguide of Ch. 2; carrier and photon confinement were introduced for LEDs in Ch. 3 and enter the threshold through Γ in Chapter 12). Thresholds dropped to a few A mm⁻², and in 1970 Hayashi and Panish, and Alferov’s group, obtained continuous operation at room temperature. Stripe contacts (slide 97) restrict the current to a few µm width; buried heterostructures (slides 98–99) surround the active region by low-index, wide-gap material on all sides, which also guides the light laterally and keeps one transverse mode.

The cavity selects longitudinal modes (slides 101–102, as in Chapter 5):

\[ m\frac{\lambda_m}{2n}=L,\qquad \Delta\lambda_m\approx\frac{\lambda^2}{2n_gL}, \]

where strictly the group index \(n_g=n-\lambda\,dn/d\lambda\approx4.3\) should replace \(n\) (the slides use \(n=3.6\)).

The threshold rises exponentially with temperature (slide 105), and the wavelength drifts (slides 106–107):

\[ I_{th}=A\exp\!\left(\frac{T}{T_0}\right),\qquad \frac{d\lambda_m}{dT}\approx\frac{\lambda_m}{n}\frac{dn}{dT}\ \text{(one mode)},\qquad \frac{d\lambda_\text{peak}}{dT}\approx-\frac{\lambda^2}{hc}\frac{dE_g}{dT}\ \text{(gain peak)}. \]

10.3 Output spectrum versus current (slides 94, 104)

Below threshold a laser diode is an LED: its spectrum follows the gain curve. Above threshold the gain clamps just below the loss, \(g_{th}\). Each mode \(m\) is then fed by spontaneous emission and amplified by its own net gain: its photon number is about \(S_m\propto1/(g_{th}-g_m)\). The mode at the gain peak gets closest to \(g_{th}\) and takes over as the current rises.

Show the Python 
viewof fs = Inputs.form({
  L: Inputs.range([100, 1000], {value: 250, step: 10, label: "cavity length L (µm)"}),
  ng: Inputs.range([3.4, 4.5], {value: 4.3, step: 0.05, label: "group index n_g"}),
  lam: Inputs.range([800, 1600], {value: 870, step: 1, label: "gain peak (nm)"}),
  fw: Inputs.range([5, 60], {value: 10, step: 1, label: "gain FWHM (nm)"}),
  off: Inputs.range([0, 0.5], {value: 0.1, step: 0.01, label: "comb position: nearest mode − gain peak (mode spacings)"}),
  I: Inputs.range([0.5, 5], {value: 1.3, step: 0.01, label: "current I/I_th"})
})
Show the Python 
fsOut = {
  const dl = fs.lam**2/(2*fs.ng*fs.L*1e3), half = fs.fw/2, gain = l => Math.max(1 - ((l - fs.lam)/half)**2*0.5, 0);
  // gain deficit of the peak below threshold: falls as 1/(I/Ith - 1) above threshold
  const delta = fs.I < 1 ? 1 - fs.I*0.9 : 1e-5/(fs.I - 1 + 0.003);
  const modes = []; const off = fs.off;   // comb offset with respect to the gain peak
  for (let k = -80; k <= 80; k++) {
    const l = fs.lam + (k + off)*dl; if (Math.abs(l - fs.lam) > 2*fs.fw) continue;
    const gm = gain(l)*(1 - delta), S = 1/(1 - gm);
    modes.push({l, S});
  }
  const mx = d3.max(modes, d => d.S), sorted = modes.map(d => d.S).sort((a, b) => b - a);
  return {modes: modes.map(d => ({...d, P: d.S/mx})), dl, smsr: 10*Math.log10(sorted[0]/sorted[1]), nm: modes.length};
}
Plot.plot({
  width: Math.min(width, 1050), height: 280, x: {label: "wavelength (nm)"}, y: {label: "relative power (each spectrum normalised)", grid: true, domain: [0, 1.05]},
  marks: [Plot.ruleX(fsOut.modes, {x: "l", y1: 0, y2: "P", stroke: "#e34948", strokeWidth: fsOut.modes.length > 150 ? 1 : 2})]
})
Show the Python 
html`<div class="result-box">mode spacing <b>${fsOut.dl.toFixed(3)} nm</b> · ${fs.I < 1 ? "below threshold: LED-like spectrum following the gain curve" : html`side-mode suppression ratio ≈ <b>${fsOut.smsr.toFixed(1)} dB</b>`}</div>`

With a mode close to the gain peak, the side-mode suppression grows with current to 20–30 dB, as on slide 104. Now move the comb so that two modes straddle the peak (position 0.5): their gains are equal and they share the power at any current. A small change of temperature or current moves the comb and flips the laser from one mode to the other (mode hopping, below). The suppression is limited by the tiny gain difference between neighbouring modes, \(\approx\tfrac12(\Delta\lambda_m/\text{half width})^2\). This is why Fabry–Perot lasers rarely exceed 20–30 dB, and why single-frequency lasers need an extra wavelength-selective element (Chapter 14).

A single mode drifts by 0.05 nm/K, while the gain peak drifts six times faster with the bandgap. Over a 20 °C range the gain peak moves by 6 nm, that is 10 mode spacings of a 250 µm laser: the laser must hop. For WDM channels spaced by 0.8 nm (Ch. 2), telecom lasers sit on a thermoelectric cooler that holds the temperature to ±0.01 K.

10.5 Beam divergence (slide 103)

The near field of an edge emitter is an ellipse about 1 µm × 3 µm. Diffraction (Ch. 1) makes the far field an ellipse rotated by 90°: the beam diverges fastest in the direction where it is narrowest. For a Gaussian beam of waist \(w_0\) the full angle at half intensity is \(\theta_\text{FWHM}\approx1.18\lambda/(\pi w_0)\) (radians):

Show the Python 
lam = 870e-9
rows = []
for name, w0 in (("perpendicular, thin DH (w₀ = 0.4 µm)", 0.4e-6), ("perpendicular, large-optical-cavity (w₀ = 0.8 µm)", 0.8e-6),
                 ("parallel, narrow ridge (w₀ = 1.5 µm)", 1.5e-6), ("parallel, broad stripe (w₀ = 3 µm)", 3e-6)):
    th = 2*np.degrees(np.arctan(np.sqrt(np.log(2)/2)*lam/(np.pi*w0)))     # full angle at half intensity
    rows.append({"direction": name, "near-field FWHM (µm)": f"{w0*np.sqrt(2*np.log(2)):.2f}", "far-field FWHM": f"{th:.0f}°"})
pd.DataFrame(rows)

A cylindrical lens or an anamorphic prism pair circularises the beam before it is coupled into a fiber (Ch. 2). VCSELs (Chapter 14) emit a circular beam directly.

TipGoing further
  • Measure the group index. The FP mode spacing of a real laser gives \(n_g\), not \(n\). Below threshold the modes are visible in the spontaneous spectrum; their contrast gives the net gain (Hakki–Paoli method).
  • Why \(T_0\) is low for InGaAsP. Auger recombination and carrier leakage over small band offsets grow quickly with temperature. 1.3 µm InGaAsP lasers have \(T_0\approx50\)–70 K, AlGaInAs lasers 80–100 K, quantum-dot lasers more than 200 K. Compute the threshold at 85 °C (the industrial specification) for each.
  • Thermal rollover. Add self-heating \(T=T_\text{case}+R_\text{th}(IV-P_o)\) to the threshold law and solve for the L–I curve. The output bends down at high current. Typical \(R_\text{th}=50\)–100 K/W.

A single mode drifts by 0.05 nm/K, while the gain peak drifts six times faster with the bandgap. Over a 20 °C range the gain peak
moves by 6 nm, that is 10 mode spacings of a 250 µm laser: the laser must hop. For WDM channels spaced by 0.8 nm (Ch. 2),
telecom lasers sit on a thermoelectric cooler that holds the temperature to ±0.01 K.

## Beam divergence (slide 103)

The near field of an edge emitter is an ellipse about 1 µm × 3 µm. Diffraction (Ch. 1) makes the far field an ellipse rotated by
90°: the beam diverges fastest in the direction where it is narrowest. For a Gaussian beam of waist $w_0$ the full angle at half
intensity is $\theta_\text{FWHM}\approx1.18\lambda/(\pi w_0)$ (radians):

```{python}
#| label: tbl-divergence
#| tbl-cap: "Far-field divergence of a Gaussian near field (FWHM full angle) for typical mode sizes of an 870 nm edge emitter. Compare with θ⊥ ≈ 17–45° and θ∥ ≈ 8–15° on slide 116."
lam = 870e-9
rows = []
for name, w0 in (("perpendicular, thin DH (w₀ = 0.4 µm)", 0.4e-6), ("perpendicular, large-optical-cavity (w₀ = 0.8 µm)", 0.8e-6),
                 ("parallel, narrow ridge (w₀ = 1.5 µm)", 1.5e-6), ("parallel, broad stripe (w₀ = 3 µm)", 3e-6)):
    th = 2*np.degrees(np.arctan(np.sqrt(np.log(2)/2)*lam/(np.pi*w0)))     # full angle at half intensity
    rows.append({"direction": name, "near-field FWHM (µm)": f"{w0*np.sqrt(2*np.log(2)):.2f}", "far-field FWHM": f"{th:.0f}°"})
pd.DataFrame(rows)
```

A cylindrical lens or an anamorphic prism pair circularises the beam before it is coupled into a fiber (Ch. 2). VCSELs
(@sec-single-mode) emit a circular beam directly.

## Going further
- **Measure the group index.** The FP mode spacing of a real laser gives $n_g$, not $n$. Below threshold the modes are visible in
  the spontaneous spectrum; their contrast gives the net gain (Hakki–Paoli method).
- **Why $T_0$ is low for InGaAsP.** Auger recombination and carrier leakage over small band offsets grow quickly with temperature.
  1.3 µm InGaAsP lasers have $T_0\approx50$–70 K, AlGaInAs lasers 80–100 K, quantum-dot lasers more than 200 K. Compute the threshold
  at 85 °C (the industrial specification) for each.
- **Thermal rollover.** Add self-heating $T=T_\text{case}+R_\text{th}(IV-P_o)$ to the threshold law and solve for the L–I curve.
  The output bends down at high current. Typical $R_\text{th}=50$–100 K/W.
:::
